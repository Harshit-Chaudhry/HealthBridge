# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Chickenpox
Medical Name: Varicella (primary infection with varicella-zoster virus, VZV)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (viral); highly contagious; childhood rash illness
Also Known As: Varicella, chicken pox, "Chhoti Mata" / "Mata" / "Cheechak" (names used in parts of India), VZV infection (the same virus later causes shingles, or herpes zoster)

## 1. Overview
Chickenpox is a highly contagious viral illness caused by the **varicella-zoster virus (VZV)**. It causes an itchy, blister-like rash that appears in "crops" over several days, often with fever, tiredness and headache.

In many places it is most common in children, and in children it is usually mild. However, chickenpox can be **more severe in newborns, adolescents, adults, pregnant women, and people with weakened immunity**, and can sometimes cause serious complications.

Chickenpox can be **prevented by vaccination**. After recovery, the virus stays dormant in the body's nerve cells for life and can reactivate years later as **shingles (herpes zoster)**.

Chickenpox is not the same as smallpox or mpox, which are caused by different viruses.

## 2. Causes
- Chickenpox is caused by the **varicella-zoster virus (VZV)**, a member of the herpesvirus family.
- The virus spreads easily from person to person, and most people who are not immune and are exposed to an infected person become infected.
- After the illness, the virus remains inactive in nerve cells. Later in life it can reactivate and cause **shingles**, especially in older adults or people with weakened immunity.
- A person with shingles can spread the virus to others who have never had chickenpox or the vaccine, causing chickenpox in them (not shingles).

## 3. Risk Factors
**Risk of infection (not immune):**
- Never having had chickenpox and not being vaccinated
- Close contact with someone with chickenpox or shingles, such as at home, school, nursery or in healthcare settings
- Living in or attending crowded places such as schools and childcare centres
- Being a child (most cases occur in childhood in many regions), though infection can occur at any age if not immune
- Being a health worker or caregiver without immunity

**Risk of severe illness:**
- Newborns and infants
- Adolescents and adults (illness tends to be more severe than in young children)
- Pregnant women who are not immune
- People with weakened immunity (for example, from HIV, cancer, chemotherapy, organ transplant, or long-term steroid or immunosuppressive medicines)
- People with chronic skin conditions or lung disease
- Smokers (higher risk of chickenpox pneumonia in adults)
- People taking certain medicines, such as long-term steroids

## 4. Transmission
- **Airborne and droplet spread:** The virus spreads through the air in tiny droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes or breathes, and can spread over some distance indoors.
- **Direct contact:** By touching or being in contact with fluid from the blisters of a person with chickenpox or shingles.
- **Contaminated items:** Less commonly, through objects recently contaminated with fluid from blisters or respiratory secretions.
- **Mother to baby:** A pregnant woman with chickenpox can pass the virus to her unborn baby, or to the newborn around the time of delivery.
- **Incubation period:** Symptoms usually start about **10 to 21 days** after exposure (commonly around 14 to 16 days).
- **Infectious period:** A person is contagious from about **1 to 2 days before the rash appears until all the blisters have crusted over** (usually about 5 to 7 days after the rash begins). People with weakened immunity can be contagious longer.
- **Contagiousness:** Chickenpox is **very contagious**. Most non-immune household contacts become infected.
- **Not spread by:** Contact with the scabs after they have formed and dried are generally not considered infectious, and the virus isn't spread by food or water.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
- **Itchy rash** that develops into fluid-filled blisters, then breaks open and crusts over into scabs. The rash typically appears in successive "crops", so spots at different stages (red spots, blisters, scabs) are present at the same time.
- The rash usually starts on the face, chest and back and then spreads to the rest of the body, including inside the mouth, scalp and sometimes the eyelids or genital area.
- Fever (usually mild to moderate)
- Tiredness and weakness
- Headache
- Loss of appetite
- General feeling of being unwell, sometimes before the rash begins

### Less Common Symptoms
- Muscle or body aches
- Sore throat or cough
- Stomach ache or nausea
- Blisters or sores in the mouth or throat, causing pain when eating
- In adults and adolescents: a more intense rash, higher fever and a longer illness
- In vaccinated people: "breakthrough" chickenpox, usually milder, with fewer spots (often fewer than 50), little or no fever, and quicker recovery

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Suspected chickenpox in a newborn, infant, pregnant woman, adult, or person with weakened immunity
- Fever that lasts more than 4 days, or rises above 102 °F (about 39 °C)
- A rash that becomes very red, warm, swollen, tender or leaks pus (possible bacterial skin infection)
- Rash spreading to the eye, or eye pain or redness
- Difficulty eating or drinking, signs of dehydration
- Cough or breathing problems
- Chickenpox in someone who has been exposed to a person who is high risk (such as a pregnant woman or a newborn at home)

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital or call local emergency services) if a person with chickenpox has:
- Difficulty breathing, fast breathing, or persistent cough with chest pain
- Severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, extreme drowsiness, difficulty waking, seizures, or loss of balance and coordination (possible brain involvement)
- Persistent vomiting
- A rash that is turning dark red or purple, or spreading skin redness, swelling, severe pain or pus, especially with high fever (possible serious bacterial skin or blood infection)
- Rash with bleeding into the skin (spots that look like bruises) or bleeding from other sites
- Signs of severe dehydration (very little urine, very dry mouth, dizziness)
- High fever that does not respond to treatment, or fever returning after it had settled
- Eye pain, redness or loss of vision
- A newborn or young infant with fever or chickenpox rash
- A pregnant woman with chickenpox, particularly with breathing difficulty

## 7. Diagnosis
- **Clinical diagnosis:** Chickenpox is usually diagnosed by a doctor based on the typical itchy blistering rash in various stages and a history of exposure.
- **Laboratory confirmation:** Tests may be used if the diagnosis is uncertain, in high-risk people, in pregnancy, in newborns, or in people who have been vaccinated (where the rash may look different). These include **PCR testing** of fluid from a blister or scab, direct fluorescent antibody tests, and **blood antibody tests** (IgM, or IgG to check for immunity).
- **Immunity check:** A blood test for VZV IgG antibodies can show whether a person (for example, a pregnant woman or health worker) is already immune.
- **Other tests:** Additional tests such as a chest X-ray, blood tests or brain imaging may be done if complications are suspected.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions, including whether an antiviral medicine is needed, are made by a doctor based on age, risk factors, and how long the person has had symptoms. Do not self-medicate.*

- **Most healthy children recover at home** within about 1 to 2 weeks. Care is supportive, aimed at relieving symptoms and preventing complications.
- **Itch relief:** Cool baths or cool wet compresses, loose cotton clothing, and keeping the skin cool. Calamine lotion or other soothing products and, if advised, an antihistamine may help. Ask a doctor or pharmacist what is suitable, especially for children.
- **Prevent scratching and infection:** Keep fingernails short and clean, and discourage scratching to reduce the risk of scarring and bacterial skin infection. Gentle washing with mild soap and drying by patting.
- **Fever and pain relief:** Doctors commonly advise **paracetamol (acetaminophen)** as directed.
- **Avoid aspirin:** **Do not give aspirin or aspirin-containing medicines to children or teenagers** with chickenpox, because of the risk of **Reye's syndrome**, a rare but serious condition affecting the liver and brain.
- **Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs:** Many medical guidelines advise avoiding ibuprofen during chickenpox unless a doctor recommends it, because of a possible link with serious skin infections. Ask a doctor before using.
- **Mouth sores:** Soft, cool foods and plenty of fluids can help if blisters in the mouth make eating painful.
- **Fluids:** Encourage plenty of fluids to prevent dehydration.
- **Antiviral medicines:** Prescription antivirals (such as aciclovir/acyclovir or valaciclovir) may be given to people at higher risk of severe illness, for example adults, adolescents, pregnant women, newborns, people with weakened immunity, and those with complications, **ideally started within about 24 hours of the rash appearing**. A doctor decides who needs them.
- **Protective antibodies after exposure:** In some high-risk people who are exposed and not immune (such as pregnant women, newborns, and people with weakened immunity), doctors may give varicella-zoster immune globulin or an antiviral to reduce the risk of severe disease. This needs prompt medical advice.
- **Isolation:** Stay home from school, work or childcare until all blisters have crusted over, and avoid contact with high-risk people (newborns, pregnant women, people with weakened immunity).
- **Hospital care:** Needed for severe disease or complications.
- **In India,** consult a doctor at the nearest health centre or hospital if the patient is a newborn, pregnant woman, adult, or person with weakened immunity, or if complications are suspected.

## 9. Prevention
- **Vaccination:** The **varicella (chickenpox) vaccine** is the best protection. It is a live, weakened-virus vaccine, usually given as **two doses** in countries with routine varicella vaccination programmes. Vaccination greatly reduces the chance of getting chickenpox and, if breakthrough illness occurs, it is usually milder. Vaccines are used in the national immunisation programmes of many countries; in some countries, including India, varicella vaccination is recommended by paediatric societies and available through doctors, and national schedules differ. Ask a doctor or local health authority about the vaccine schedule and availability.
- **Who should not receive the vaccine:** Live vaccines are generally not given during pregnancy or to people with severely weakened immunity. People should check with a doctor about suitability. Women should avoid pregnancy for a period after vaccination, as advised by a doctor.
- **Check immunity:** Women planning pregnancy, health workers and others at risk who have no history of chickenpox or vaccination should ask a doctor about immunity testing and vaccination.
- **Isolation of cases:** Keep infected people at home until all blisters have crusted over.
- **Avoid contact with infected people** if you are not immune, and especially if you are pregnant, a newborn, or have weakened immunity.
- **Hygiene:** Wash hands frequently, cover coughs and sneezes, and keep rooms well ventilated. Chickenpox is spread mainly through the air, so these measures help but do not fully prevent spread.
- **Post-exposure vaccination:** In some situations, the vaccine given soon after exposure (within a few days) can prevent or reduce illness in people who are not immune and are eligible. A doctor decides.
- **Shingles prevention:** A separate vaccine is available for preventing shingles in older adults and some at-risk people. Ask a doctor for advice.
- **Avoid "chickenpox parties":** Deliberately exposing children to chickenpox is not recommended because the illness can be serious and unpredictable, and vaccination is a safer way to build protection.

## 10. Complications
Most children recover without problems, but complications can occur, especially in newborns, adults and people with weakened immunity.
- **Bacterial skin infections:** The most common complication, from scratching or open blisters (such as impetigo or cellulitis), which can leave scars. Rarely, severe infections such as necrotising fasciitis or toxic shock syndrome.
- **Pneumonia:** Viral pneumonia, which is more common and more severe in adults and pregnant women
- **Neurological complications:** Encephalitis (brain inflammation), cerebellar ataxia (balance and coordination problems), meningitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome
- **Reye's syndrome:** In children and teenagers given aspirin
- **Dehydration**
- **Bleeding problems and low platelets**
- **Complications in pregnancy:** Risk of severe illness in the mother (especially pneumonia), and **congenital varicella syndrome** in the baby if infected during pregnancy (including skin scarring, limb, eye and brain problems), or severe newborn infection if the mother develops chickenpox shortly before or after delivery
- **Severe or disseminated illness** in people with weakened immunity
- **Secondary bacterial infection of the blood, bones or joints**
- **Shingles later in life:** The virus remains dormant after chickenpox and can reactivate as shingles
- **Death:** Rare, but can occur, particularly in adults, newborns and people with weakened immunity

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Newborns and infants
- Pregnant women who have never had chickenpox or the vaccine
- Adolescents and adults who are not immune
- People with weakened immunity (HIV, cancer, chemotherapy, organ transplant, long-term steroid or immunosuppressive medicines)
- People with chronic lung or skin diseases
- People in close contact with high-risk people (for example, family members of newborns or immunocompromised people)
- Health workers who are not immune
- People who are unvaccinated and have no history of chickenpox
- People in crowded settings such as schools, childcare, dormitories, hostels and prisons

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Suspected chickenpox in a newborn, infant, pregnant woman, adult, teenager, or person with weakened immunity. **Contact a doctor promptly**, since antiviral treatment works best when started early.
- You are pregnant or have a newborn at home, and you have been in contact with someone with chickenpox or shingles
- Fever lasting more than 4 days, or fever returning after settling
- Rash that becomes red, swollen, warm, painful or oozing pus
- Rash near or in the eye
- Cough, breathing difficulty, severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, unsteadiness, seizures, or persistent vomiting. **Go to hospital immediately** (see Section 6).
- Signs of dehydration, or unable to drink
- Deciding about chickenpox vaccination for yourself or your child
- Before giving any medicine to a child with chickenpox, including painkillers or itch-relief medicines
- If you are unsure whether the rash is chickenpox

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Chickenpox is always mild and harmless."** It is often mild in children, but it can cause serious complications and, rarely, death, particularly in newborns, adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immunity.
2. **"It is better to catch chickenpox as a child on purpose."** Deliberate exposure (such as "chickenpox parties") is not recommended because the illness can be unpredictable and serious. Vaccination is safer.
3. **"You can never get chickenpox twice."** Second infections are uncommon but possible. After chickenpox, the virus can reactivate as shingles.
4. **"Chickenpox is caused by a curse, goddess's anger or poor hygiene."** Chickenpox is an infection caused by a virus. Early medical advice and proper care matter. Cultural practices should not replace medical care.
5. **"Vaccinated people can't get chickenpox."** Breakthrough infections can occur, but they are usually milder.
6. **"Scratching spreads the rash to other body parts."** Scratching does not spread the virus around the body (it spreads through the bloodstream), but it can cause bacterial infection and scars.
7. **"Aspirin is fine for the fever."** Aspirin should not be given to children and teenagers with chickenpox because of the risk of Reye's syndrome.
8. **"Chickenpox and smallpox (or mpox) are the same."** They are caused by different viruses and are different diseases.
9. **"Once the fever is gone, the person is no longer contagious."** A person with chickenpox remains contagious until all the blisters have crusted over.
10. **"Only children get chickenpox."** Adults who have never had chickenpox or the vaccine can get it, and the illness is often more severe in adults.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is chickenpox?**
Chickenpox is a highly contagious viral illness caused by the varicella-zoster virus. It causes an itchy blistering rash, often with fever and tiredness.

**Q2. How does chickenpox spread?**
Through the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes or breathes, and through direct contact with fluid from the blisters. People with shingles can also spread the virus to non-immune people.

**Q3. How long after exposure do symptoms appear?**
Usually about 10 to 21 days after exposure, often around 2 weeks.

**Q4. When is a person with chickenpox contagious?**
From about 1 to 2 days before the rash appears until all blisters have crusted over, usually about 5 to 7 days after the rash starts.

**Q5. What does the chickenpox rash look like?**
It starts as itchy red spots that turn into fluid-filled blisters, then break and crust into scabs. New spots keep appearing for several days, so spots in different stages are present at once.

**Q6. How is chickenpox treated?**
Mostly at home with rest, fluids, and care for itch and fever as advised by a doctor. Antiviral medicines may be prescribed for people at higher risk. Do not self-medicate.

**Q7. Can I give aspirin to a child with chickenpox?**
No. Aspirin should not be given to children and teenagers with chickenpox because of the risk of Reye's syndrome. Always ask a doctor or pharmacist before giving a child any medicine.

**Q8. How can I relieve itching?**
Cool baths, cool compresses, loose cotton clothing, short clean fingernails, and soothing lotions such as calamine. A doctor or pharmacist may advise other options.

**Q9. When can my child go back to school?**
When all the blisters have crusted over and the child feels well, as advised by a doctor and local school rules.

**Q10. Is there a vaccine for chickenpox?**
Yes. The varicella vaccine is usually given as two doses. It greatly lowers the chance of getting chickenpox and makes illness milder if it happens. Ask a doctor about the schedule and availability.

**Q11. Is chickenpox dangerous in pregnancy?**
It can be. A pregnant woman who has chickenpox can become seriously ill (for example, with pneumonia), and the baby can be affected. Contact a doctor immediately if you are pregnant and have been exposed or have symptoms.

**Q12. What is the connection between chickenpox and shingles?**
Both are caused by the same virus. After chickenpox, the virus stays dormant in the body and can reactivate years later as shingles, a painful blistering rash.

**Q13. Can a person who has been vaccinated still get chickenpox?**
Yes, but it is usually much milder, with fewer spots and a shorter illness.

**Q14. Can adults get chickenpox?**
Yes, if they have never had chickenpox or the vaccine. Illness is often more severe in adults, so medical advice should be sought early.

**Q15. Where can I get chickenpox care and vaccination in India?**
At government and private health facilities, including primary health centres, hospitals and paediatric clinics. Ask a doctor about diagnosis, treatment and the vaccination schedule for your child or yourself.
