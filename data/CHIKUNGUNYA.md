# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Chikungunya
Medical Name: Chikungunya virus disease (caused by chikungunya virus, CHIKV)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (viral); mosquito-borne (vector-borne)
Also Known As: Chikungunya fever, CHIKV infection, chik (informal)

## 1. Overview
Chikungunya is a viral illness spread to people by the bite of infected *Aedes* mosquitoes. It causes a sudden fever and, characteristically, **severe joint pain**. The name comes from a word in the Kimakonde language of southeastern Tanzania, often translated as "to become contorted" or "that which bends up", describing the stooped posture of people with the joint pain.

The disease is found in parts of Africa, Asia (including India), the Americas, and some areas of Europe and the Indian and Pacific Ocean regions. Most people recover completely, usually within a week or two. However, in some people the joint pain can last for weeks, months or longer. Deaths are uncommon, but severe illness can occur in newborns, older adults and people with other medical conditions.

There is no specific antiviral treatment for chikungunya. Care focuses on relieving symptoms, and prevention depends mainly on avoiding mosquito bites and controlling mosquito breeding.

## 2. Causes
- Chikungunya is caused by the **chikungunya virus (CHIKV)**, an RNA virus belonging to the genus *Alphavirus* (family Togaviridae).
- It was first identified in Tanzania in the early 1950s and has since spread to many regions.
- The virus is carried by mosquitoes, not by food, water or air.

## 3. Risk Factors
- Living in or travelling to areas where chikungunya is circulating
- Being bitten by *Aedes* mosquitoes (they are common in and around homes in urban and semi-urban areas)
- Stagnant water near homes where mosquitoes breed: containers, coolers, tyres, flower pots, water tanks and construction sites
- Not using mosquito protection (repellents, screens, protective clothing)
- Rainy season and warm, humid conditions that favour mosquito breeding
- Crowded living conditions and poor water storage
- Having no previous infection (the population is generally susceptible when the virus first arrives in an area)
- For severe illness, see High-Risk Groups

## 4. Transmission
- **Main route:** The bite of an infected female *Aedes* mosquito, mainly *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus* (the "Asian tiger mosquito").
- **Biting time:** These mosquitoes bite mainly during the **daytime**, with peaks in early morning and late afternoon. They can also bite outdoors and indoors.
- **Cycle:** A mosquito becomes infected by biting a person with the virus in their blood, usually in the first week of illness. It can then pass the virus on to other people.
- **Incubation period in humans:** Symptoms usually begin about 3 to 7 days after the bite, though the range can be wider (up to about 12 days).
- **Other (rare) routes:** From mother to newborn when the mother is infected around the time of delivery. Very rarely through blood transfusion or laboratory exposure.
- **Not spread by:** Casual contact, coughing, sneezing, touching, or sharing food.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
- Sudden fever (often high)
- **Severe joint pain**, often in the hands, wrists, ankles, feet and knees, usually on both sides of the body
- Joint swelling and stiffness
- Headache
- Muscle pain
- Tiredness and weakness
- Nausea and vomiting
- Skin rash

### Less Common Symptoms
- Conjunctivitis (red eyes)
- Swollen glands
- Mouth ulcers
- Mild bleeding such as nosebleed (uncommon)
- Loss of appetite
- Eye pain or sensitivity to light
- Skin darkening or blisters (seen more in infants)
- Persistent joint pain, stiffness or swelling for weeks to months (chronic phase)

Some infected people have no symptoms or only mild symptoms. Because chikungunya looks like dengue and Zika, a blood test is needed to tell them apart.

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Fever with severe joint pain, especially in or after a visit to an area where chikungunya occurs
- Fever with rash
- Fever in pregnant women, newborns, young children or older adults
- Joint pain that continues, worsens, or does not settle after the fever has gone
- Difficulty drinking, eating, or walking
- Fever that lasts more than a couple of days

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital) if a person with suspected chikungunya has:
- Confusion, severe drowsiness, seizures, or severe headache with neck stiffness
- Weakness or numbness spreading in the limbs, difficulty moving or walking, or difficulty breathing (possible nerve complications)
- Chest pain, palpitations or fainting (possible heart involvement)
- Persistent vomiting or inability to drink fluids
- Signs of dehydration (very dry mouth, little or no urine, dizziness)
- Bleeding, black stools, or vomiting blood
- Cold, clammy, pale skin, or extreme weakness
- A newborn or young infant with fever, poor feeding, floppiness, seizures or unusual irritability
- Severe or worsening illness in an older adult or someone with a long-term condition

Because chikungunya and dengue look similar and can occur at the same time, any of the dengue warning signs (severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, rapid breathing) also need urgent evaluation.

## 7. Diagnosis
Chikungunya is diagnosed by a healthcare professional based on symptoms, exposure history and laboratory tests.
- **RT-PCR (molecular test):** Detects virus genetic material in blood, most useful in the **first days** (roughly the first week) of illness.
- **Serology (IgM and IgG antibody tests):** Detect the immune response; IgM usually becomes detectable from around the end of the first week. Results must be interpreted with the timing of illness.
- **Other tests:** Blood counts and other tests may be done to rule out dengue, malaria and other illnesses that cause fever, or to monitor complications.
- **Differential diagnosis:** Chikungunya can resemble dengue and Zika, and these infections can occur together in the same place and even in the same person. Doctors use testing to tell them apart, because the treatment advice differs (for example, regarding which painkillers are safe).

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions are made by a doctor based on the person's age, condition and stage of illness. Do not self-medicate.*

- **No specific antiviral treatment exists.** Care is supportive.
- **Rest and fluids:** Resting and drinking enough fluids helps prevent dehydration.
- **Fever and pain relief:** Doctors commonly advise **paracetamol (acetaminophen)** for fever and pain, as directed.
- **Painkillers to check first:** **Aspirin and NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen)** are generally avoided in the early feverish phase until dengue has been ruled out, because of bleeding risk in dengue. After dengue is excluded, a doctor may advise other pain relief for joint pain. Do not start any painkiller without medical advice.
- **Joint pain management:** For persistent joint pain, doctors may suggest approaches such as rest, gentle movement, physiotherapy and, where suitable, other medicines. People with ongoing joint pain or swelling should see a doctor, as some need specialist (for example, rheumatology) care.
- **Hospital care:** Needed for severe symptoms, dehydration, complications, newborns, or people with other serious conditions.
- **Prevent spreading to others:** During the first week of illness, the person's blood can infect mosquitoes. Use mosquito nets, screens and repellents so mosquitoes do not bite the patient and carry the virus to others.
- **Recovery:** Many people feel better in about 1 to 2 weeks. Joint pain and tiredness can last longer, sometimes for months or beyond.
- **In India,** testing and care are available at government health facilities under national vector-borne disease control guidelines. Ask at your nearest health centre or hospital.

## 9. Prevention
Mosquito control and avoiding bites are the main ways to prevent chikungunya.
- **Remove standing water** around the home: empty and scrub water containers, coolers, flower vases, plant saucers, buckets and old tyres at least once a week; cover water tanks and storage containers tightly.
- **Prevent bites (especially during the day):** Use mosquito repellents (as recommended by health authorities), wear long sleeves and trousers, and use window and door screens, mosquito nets for people resting or sleeping by day, and coils or vaporisers.
- **Protect sick people:** Keep infected people away from mosquito bites in the first week of illness.
- **Community action:** Join local clean-up, larval control and fogging campaigns organised by health authorities.
- **Travellers:** Use bite protection during the day when visiting affected areas, and see a doctor if fever and joint pain develop during or after travel.
- **Vaccines:** Chikungunya vaccines have been licensed in some countries in recent years. Their use, availability and safety recommendations (especially for older adults and people with certain health conditions) have been changing, and differ by country. Ask a doctor, travel clinic or the national health authority about the current situation. Vaccination does not replace mosquito protection.
- **Pregnancy:** Pregnant women near delivery in an outbreak area should seek medical advice on protecting themselves and the newborn.

## 10. Complications
- **Chronic joint pain and arthritis:** Joint pain, stiffness and swelling that can persist for weeks, months or longer in some people, affecting daily activities and work.
- **Neurological complications (uncommon):** Inflammation of the brain (encephalitis), meningitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome and nerve problems.
- **Heart complications (uncommon):** Inflammation of the heart muscle or irregular heartbeat.
- **Eye complications (uncommon):** Inflammation of the eye (such as uveitis or retinitis).
- **Skin and kidney complications (uncommon)**
- **Severe illness in newborns:** Babies infected around birth may develop fever, poor feeding, seizures, and brain or heart problems.
- **Worsening of existing conditions** such as diabetes, heart disease or kidney disease.
- **Dehydration** from fever and vomiting.
- **Death** is uncommon, but can occur, particularly in newborns, older adults and people with other serious illness.

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Newborns infected around the time of birth
- Older adults (generally those over about 60 or 65 years)
- People with chronic conditions such as diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, kidney disease or chronic lung disease
- Pregnant women (especially near delivery, because of risk to the baby)
- People with weakened immunity
- Young children and infants
- People living in crowded areas with poor water storage and mosquito control
- People in or travelling to areas with outbreaks
- People with limited access to healthcare

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Sudden fever with severe joint pain, especially if you live in or have recently travelled to an area where chikungunya or dengue occurs
- Fever with rash and joint pain
- Fever in a pregnant woman, newborn, infant, young child or older adult
- Joint pain, swelling or stiffness that continues for more than a couple of weeks, or that interferes with daily life
- Difficulty drinking fluids, repeated vomiting, or signs of dehydration
- Any warning sign in Section 6 (**go to hospital immediately**)
- Before taking painkillers for fever and joint pain, to avoid medicines that may be unsafe if the illness is actually dengue
- Any doubts about the diagnosis, since chikungunya, dengue and Zika can look alike

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Chikungunya spreads from person to person."** It spreads through the bite of infected mosquitoes, not by casual contact.
2. **"Chikungunya mosquitoes bite only at night."** The main carrier mosquitoes bite mostly during the day.
3. **"Chikungunya is the same as dengue."** They are different viruses, though symptoms overlap. Tests can distinguish them, and some safety advice (such as painkillers) differs in the early stage.
4. **"The joint pain goes away completely within days."** Many people recover within weeks, but in some the joint pain lasts for months or longer.
5. **"Chikungunya is always mild."** Most cases recover, but severe illness can occur in newborns, older adults and people with other conditions.
6. **"Home remedies cure chikungunya."** No home remedy has been proven to cure it. Seek medical care and discuss any supplements or traditional remedies with a doctor.
7. **"I can take any painkiller for the pain."** Aspirin and NSAIDs may be unsafe until dengue has been ruled out. Ask a doctor first.
8. **"You can only get it once, so I need no precautions."** Infection is believed to give long-lasting immunity, but mosquito control still matters to protect others and prevent other mosquito-borne diseases.
9. **"Chikungunya is only a problem in the rainy season."** Cases often rise in and after rains, but mosquitoes can breed whenever there is standing water.
10. **"Chikungunya cannot be prevented."** Removing standing water, using repellents, screens and nets, and community mosquito control all help prevent it.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is chikungunya?**
Chikungunya is a viral illness spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes. It causes sudden fever and severe joint pain, which can last a long time in some people.

**Q2. How does chikungunya spread?**
Through the bite of an infected *Aedes* mosquito (mainly *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus*). It does not spread directly from person to person through touch or coughing.

**Q3. What are the symptoms of chikungunya?**
Sudden fever, severe joint pain (especially in hands, wrists, ankles and feet), joint swelling, headache, muscle pain, tiredness, nausea and rash.

**Q4. How soon after a bite do symptoms start?**
Usually about 3 to 7 days, but it can range up to about 12 days.

**Q5. How is chikungunya different from dengue?**
Both spread through *Aedes* mosquitoes and cause fever and body pain. Chikungunya typically causes more intense and longer-lasting joint pain, while dengue is more likely to cause dangerous bleeding, low platelets and shock. Blood tests are needed to tell them apart, and they can occur together.

**Q6. How is chikungunya diagnosed?**
By a doctor's assessment and laboratory tests, mainly RT-PCR in the first days of illness and antibody (IgM/IgG) tests later.

**Q7. Is there a specific treatment or cure?**
There is no specific antiviral medicine. Treatment is supportive: rest, fluids, and pain and fever relief as advised by a doctor.

**Q8. Which painkillers are safe?**
Doctors commonly advise paracetamol for fever and pain. Aspirin and NSAIDs are generally avoided in the early feverish stage until dengue is ruled out. Always check with a doctor before taking any medicine.

**Q9. How long does the joint pain last?**
In many people it settles within one to two weeks. In some, it can continue for weeks or months, or longer, and may need medical follow-up.

**Q10. Can chikungunya be fatal?**
Death is uncommon, but severe illness can occur, particularly in newborns, older adults and people with other medical conditions.

**Q11. Can I get chikungunya more than once?**
Infection is generally thought to provide long-lasting immunity, so repeat infection appears uncommon. You should still protect yourself from mosquito bites because of other mosquito-borne diseases such as dengue.

**Q12. How can I prevent chikungunya?**
Remove standing water weekly, cover water containers, use repellents, screens and nets, wear protective clothing, and support community mosquito control.

**Q13. Is there a vaccine for chikungunya?**
Vaccines have been licensed in some countries in recent years, but availability, approved age groups and safety guidance differ and have been changing. Ask a doctor, travel clinic or local health authority for current information. Mosquito protection remains essential.

**Q14. Can a pregnant woman pass chikungunya to her baby?**
Mother-to-child transmission is uncommon but can happen, mainly when the mother is infected around the time of delivery. Newborns infected this way can become seriously ill. Pregnant women should seek medical advice if they have fever and joint pain.

**Q15. Where can I get chikungunya testing and care in India?**
At government health facilities such as primary health centres and hospitals, which follow national vector-borne disease control guidelines. Ask at your nearest health centre or a local health worker.
