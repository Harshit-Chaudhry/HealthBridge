# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Cholera
Medical Name: Cholera (caused by toxigenic *Vibrio cholerae* serogroups O1 and O139)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (bacterial); water-borne and food-borne; acute diarrhoeal disease
Also Known As: Asiatic cholera, epidemic cholera, *Vibrio cholerae* infection

## 1. Overview
Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal illness caused by infection of the intestine with the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae*. It spreads through contaminated water and food, and is a disease of poor sanitation and unsafe drinking water.

Most infected people have no symptoms or only mild symptoms. In a minority, cholera can cause **severe watery diarrhoea and vomiting that quickly lead to dangerous dehydration**. Without prompt treatment, severe cholera can kill within hours. With fast rehydration, most people recover, and treatment is simple and inexpensive.

Cholera still occurs in many parts of the world, especially where clean water, sanitation and hygiene are limited, in crowded settings, and during floods, conflicts and humanitarian emergencies. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cholera remains a global public health threat and an indicator of inequity and lack of social development. See WHO's current pages for the latest figures and outbreak updates.

## 2. Causes
- Cholera is caused by the bacterium ***Vibrio cholerae***. Two serogroups, **O1 and O139**, cause epidemics. O1 is the more common.
- The bacteria produce a toxin in the small intestine that makes the intestine release large amounts of water and salts, causing watery diarrhoea.
- *V. cholerae* lives in water, especially in brackish or coastal waters and rivers, and can survive in the environment.
- Infection occurs when a person swallows food or water contaminated with the bacteria, usually from the faeces of an infected person.

## 3. Risk Factors
- Lack of safe drinking water and poor sanitation
- Living in crowded settings such as slums, camps for displaced people, or areas with poor sewage systems
- Floods, cyclones, conflict or other emergencies that damage water and sanitation systems
- Drinking untreated or contaminated water, including ice made from it
- Eating raw or undercooked seafood, especially shellfish from contaminated waters, or food washed or prepared with contaminated water
- Poor hand hygiene, especially after using the toilet and before handling food
- Close contact with someone who has cholera (through contaminated hands, food or surfaces)
- Low stomach acid (for example, from some medicines or conditions), which makes infection more likely
- Blood group O, which is associated with a higher risk of severe disease
- Limited access to healthcare and oral rehydration treatment
- Living in or travelling to areas with active cholera outbreaks

## 4. Transmission
- **Faecal-oral route:** Bacteria leave the body in the faeces (and vomit) of infected people. If these contaminate water, food, hands or surfaces, others can be infected when they swallow the bacteria.
- **Contaminated water:** The most important source, particularly where sewage leaks into drinking water.
- **Contaminated food:** Food prepared with contaminated water, handled with unwashed hands, or raw or undercooked seafood and shellfish from contaminated waters.
- **Household spread:** Cholera can spread among family members through contaminated hands, food, water or surfaces, especially if hygiene is poor. Casual contact (such as touching or being near someone) does not usually spread cholera on its own; the risk comes from contact with faeces or vomit.
- **Incubation period:** Symptoms usually begin from a few hours to 5 days after infection (often 1 to 3 days).
- **Infectious period:** An infected person, including someone without symptoms, can shed bacteria in their stool for a short time (usually 1 to 10 days), and this can contaminate the environment.
- **Not spread by:** Air, coughing or sneezing.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
Most infected people have no symptoms or only mild illness. When illness occurs:
- Sudden, painless watery diarrhoea, which can be profuse and look like "rice-water" (pale, milky, cloudy fluid)
- Vomiting
- Thirst
- Leg cramps
- Restlessness or irritability
- Fast heartbeat
- Dry mouth and reduced tears and urine (signs of dehydration)

### Less Common Symptoms
- Mild fever (fever is unusual in cholera)
- Mild abdominal discomfort
- Sunken eyes and sunken soft spot (fontanelle) in babies
- Dry, wrinkled skin and reduced skin elasticity
- Low blood sugar, especially in children
- Muscle weakness and tiredness

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Sudden watery diarrhoea, especially "rice-water" stools
- Diarrhoea with vomiting, particularly in an area with a cholera outbreak
- Signs of dehydration: dry mouth, intense thirst, sunken eyes, reduced urination, dizziness
- Diarrhoea in infants, young children, pregnant women, or older adults
- Diarrhoea after travel to, or eating food or water from, a cholera-affected area
- Diarrhoea that is frequent and large in volume, or that does not improve

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Cholera can cause severe dehydration and death within hours. **Seek urgent medical care immediately** (go to the nearest health facility or hospital), and start oral rehydration solution (ORS) on the way, if the person is able to drink, when there are:
- Very frequent or large-volume watery diarrhoea, especially rice-water stools
- Persistent or severe vomiting, or unable to drink
- Very dry mouth and tongue, sunken eyes, very little or no urine, no tears when crying
- Drowsiness, extreme weakness, floppiness, confusion or unconsciousness
- Cold, clammy, pale or bluish skin, cold hands and feet
- Rapid, weak pulse, rapid breathing, or fainting (signs of shock)
- Severe muscle cramps
- Seizures (particularly in children, which can be caused by low blood sugar)
- A baby or young child with sunken soft spot on the head, lethargy, or refusal to feed

## 7. Diagnosis
Cholera is suspected from symptoms and from the setting (for example, an outbreak or travel to an affected area), and is confirmed by laboratory testing. **Treatment should not be delayed while waiting for test results.**
- **Stool culture:** Growing *Vibrio cholerae* from a stool or rectal swab sample confirms the diagnosis and allows antibiotic sensitivity testing.
- **Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs):** Quick tests that can detect cholera antigen in stool. They are used mainly to help identify and track outbreaks, and positive results are usually confirmed by culture or molecular tests.
- **PCR (molecular testing):** Detects the genetic material of the bacteria or its toxin.
- **Other tests:** Blood tests (salts, kidney function, blood sugar) to assess dehydration and complications in severe cases.
- **Reporting:** Cholera is a notifiable disease in many countries. Health authorities investigate cases to control the spread.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions, including fluid volumes and any antibiotics, are made by healthcare professionals based on the person's age, weight, level of dehydration and local guidelines. Do not self-medicate.*

- **Rehydration is the most important treatment.** Cholera is treated by quickly replacing the water and salts lost.
- **Oral rehydration solution (ORS):** Most people with cholera can be treated successfully with ORS, a mix of clean water, salts and sugar, which is taken by mouth. WHO describes ORS as effective for the large majority of patients. ORS should be started as soon as diarrhoea begins, and continued while seeking care.
- **Intravenous (IV) fluids:** People with severe dehydration, shock, or who cannot drink need IV fluids in a health facility, often together with ORS as they improve.
- **Antibiotics:** For severe cases, an appropriate antibiotic can shorten the illness, reduce the volume of diarrhoea and reduce the time bacteria are shed. Antibiotics are chosen by a doctor, taking into account local resistance patterns. Antibiotics are an addition to, **not a substitute for**, rehydration.
- **Zinc for children:** WHO recommends zinc supplementation for children with diarrhoea, as advised by a healthcare worker.
- **Continue feeding:** Keep eating normally as soon as vomiting stops. Continue breastfeeding for infants and young children.
- **Close monitoring:** Severe cases need continuous monitoring of fluids in and out, blood sugar (especially in children), and other complications.
- **Infection control:** Wash hands thoroughly, handle faeces and vomit safely, and disinfect soiled surfaces and clothing as advised by health workers, to protect household members.
- **Recovery:** With prompt treatment, most people recover within a few days. Cholera can be fatal if treatment is delayed.
- **In India,** cholera diagnosis and treatment are available at government health facilities. Seek care at the nearest health centre or hospital without delay.

## 9. Prevention
- **Safe drinking water:** Drink boiled, filtered, bottled or properly chlorinated water. Store water in clean, covered containers.
- **Sanitation:** Use toilets and safe sewage disposal; avoid open defecation; protect water sources from contamination.
- **Hand hygiene:** Wash hands thoroughly with soap and safe water after using the toilet, after cleaning a child, and before preparing or eating food.
- **Food safety:** Eat food that is thoroughly cooked and served hot; avoid raw or undercooked seafood and shellfish in affected areas; wash and peel raw fruits and vegetables with safe water; keep food covered; avoid ice and drinks of unknown water source.
- **Prompt treatment of cases:** Early treatment with ORS reduces spread and deaths. Report suspected cases to health authorities.
- **Safe handling of faeces and vomit:** Dispose of safely and clean contaminated surfaces and clothing.
- **Oral cholera vaccines (OCVs):** WHO-prequalified oral vaccines exist and are used with other measures, mainly for people living in areas with cholera outbreaks or high risk, and for some travellers. Vaccines provide protection that is not complete and not permanent. They are used **alongside**, not instead of, safe water, sanitation and hygiene. Ask a doctor or local health authority about vaccine availability and eligibility.
- **Community action:** Health education on hygiene, protecting water sources, and support for water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure are the most lasting protections.
- **Travellers:** Follow safe food and water advice when visiting affected areas.

## 10. Complications
- **Severe dehydration** with loss of water and body salts
- **Hypovolaemic shock** (very low blood volume and blood pressure)
- **Kidney failure** from dehydration
- **Electrolyte imbalance:** Low potassium, which can cause weakness and heart rhythm problems; low sodium or acid-base disturbance
- **Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia):** Especially in children, which can cause drowsiness, seizures or coma
- **Muscle cramps and weakness**
- **Death:** Can occur within hours if severe cholera is not treated promptly. With early rehydration, the risk of death is much lower
- **Malnutrition and growth problems** in children after repeated diarrhoea
- **Complications in pregnancy:** Risk to both mother and baby

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- People living in areas without safe water and sanitation
- Young children (they dehydrate quickly)
- Infants
- Pregnant women
- Older adults
- People with malnutrition or weakened immunity (such as HIV)
- People with low stomach acid
- People living in crowded settings, camps, slums or flood-affected areas
- People in or travelling to areas with cholera outbreaks
- Household members and caregivers of people with cholera
- Food handlers who prepare food with unsafe water or poor hygiene
- People with limited access to healthcare or ORS

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Sudden watery diarrhoea, especially if profuse or "rice-water" in appearance, with or without vomiting
- Signs of dehydration: intense thirst, dry mouth, sunken eyes, reduced urine, dizziness, weakness
- Diarrhoea in an infant, young child, pregnant woman or older adult
- Diarrhoea during or after travel to, or living in, an area with cholera
- Diarrhoea lasting more than a day or two, or worsening
- Persistent vomiting or being unable to drink
- Any warning sign in Section 6 (**go to the nearest health facility immediately**)
- Meanwhile, begin ORS if the person can drink, and continue drinking small, frequent sips
- If someone in your household has been diagnosed with cholera, ask the health worker about protecting the rest of the family

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Cholera spreads through the air."** It spreads through contaminated water and food, not through the air.
2. **"You can catch cholera just by being near a sick person."** Casual contact does not usually spread it; the risk is from contact with faeces or vomit, through contaminated hands, food, water or surfaces.
3. **"Antibiotics are the main treatment."** Rapid rehydration (ORS or IV fluids) is the main treatment. Antibiotics are added in some cases and do not replace fluids.
4. **"A person with diarrhoea should stop drinking to reduce diarrhoea."** The opposite is true. People with cholera must drink plenty of ORS and safe fluids to replace what is lost.
5. **"Cholera always kills."** Most infections are mild or without symptoms. Severe cholera can be fatal, but treatment is simple and very effective when started early.
6. **"Cholera is only a problem of the past."** It still occurs in many parts of the world, especially where safe water and sanitation are lacking.
7. **"Vaccines make hygiene unnecessary."** Cholera vaccines give partial and time-limited protection. Safe water, sanitation and hygiene remain essential.
8. **"Only the very poor get cholera."** Anyone who swallows contaminated food or water can be infected, though poor sanitation raises the risk.
9. **"Cholera is just ordinary diarrhoea."** Cholera can cause extremely rapid fluid loss and shock, so it needs urgent attention and a high index of suspicion in outbreaks.
10. **"Home remedies can cure cholera."** No home remedy replaces rehydration and medical care. Home-prepared ORS from a proper recipe, or ORS packets, are used for rehydration while seeking medical care.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is cholera?**
Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal infection caused by the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae*, spread through contaminated water and food. It can cause severe dehydration.

**Q2. How does cholera spread?**
By swallowing food or water contaminated with the faeces of an infected person. Poor sanitation, unsafe water and poor hand hygiene are the main drivers.

**Q3. What are the symptoms of cholera?**
Sudden, painless watery diarrhoea (often "rice-water" in appearance), vomiting, thirst and leg cramps. Many infected people have no symptoms or only mild ones.

**Q4. How soon after infection do symptoms start?**
From a few hours to about 5 days, often within 1 to 3 days.

**Q5. Why is cholera dangerous?**
It can cause very rapid loss of body fluids and salts, leading to severe dehydration, shock and death within hours if untreated.

**Q6. What is the treatment for cholera?**
Immediate rehydration with oral rehydration solution (ORS), and IV fluids for severe cases. A doctor may also prescribe an antibiotic for severe disease. Children may be given zinc as advised by a healthcare worker.

**Q7. Do I need antibiotics for cholera?**
Not always. Rehydration is the main treatment, and antibiotics are used in some cases, usually severe ones, as decided by a doctor. Do not self-medicate with antibiotics.

**Q8. What is ORS and how do I use it?**
ORS (oral rehydration solution) is a mix of clean water, salts and sugar that replaces lost fluids. Prepare it exactly as the packet instructs, using safe water, and give small, frequent sips. A health worker can advise how much to give, based on age and degree of dehydration.

**Q9. Can I still eat and drink if I have cholera?**
Yes. Keep taking ORS and safe fluids, and resume normal eating as soon as vomiting settles. Continue breastfeeding infants.

**Q10. How is cholera diagnosed?**
By testing a stool or rectal swab sample through culture, PCR, or rapid tests. Treatment should begin immediately and not wait for results.

**Q11. How can I protect my family from cholera?**
Use safe drinking water, wash hands with soap, cook food thoroughly, avoid raw seafood in affected areas, use toilets and dispose of waste safely, and seek care early for diarrhoea.

**Q12. Is there a cholera vaccine?**
Yes. Oral cholera vaccines are used in areas with cholera outbreaks or high risk and for some travellers. Protection is partial and temporary, and vaccines do not replace safe water and hygiene. Ask your doctor or local health authority about availability.

**Q13. Can children and pregnant women get cholera?**
Yes. Children dehydrate very quickly, and pregnant women are at higher risk of complications. Both need prompt medical attention if they have severe watery diarrhoea.

**Q14. Can I get cholera again after recovering?**
Yes, reinfection is possible, particularly if exposure to contaminated water or food continues. Natural immunity may not last long.

**Q15. Where should I go if I suspect cholera in India?**
Go immediately to the nearest government health centre or hospital, and start ORS if the person can drink. Public health authorities should be informed so they can respond to outbreaks.
