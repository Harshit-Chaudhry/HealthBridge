# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Dengue
Medical Name: Dengue fever / Dengue virus infection (severe form: Severe Dengue, formerly called Dengue Haemorrhagic Fever and Dengue Shock Syndrome)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (viral); mosquito-borne (vector-borne)
Also Known As: Dengue fever, breakbone fever (informal term), dengue virus infection, DF, DHF (older term), DSS (older term)

## 1. Overview
Dengue is a viral infection spread to people through the bite of infected *Aedes* mosquitoes. It is found mainly in tropical and subtropical regions, including India, and is a growing public health problem worldwide.

Most people with dengue have no symptoms or only mild illness. Others develop a sudden high fever with severe aches and pains. A small proportion develop **severe dengue**, which can cause bleeding, organ problems and shock, and can be life-threatening without prompt medical care.

There is no specific antiviral medicine for dengue. Early recognition, rest, fluids and careful medical monitoring greatly reduce the risk of death. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of dengue cases reported globally has risen sharply over recent decades. See WHO's current dengue pages for the latest figures.

## 2. Causes
- Dengue is caused by the **dengue virus (DENV)**, a member of the *Flavivirus* family.
- There are **four closely related types (serotypes): DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3 and DENV-4**.
- Recovery from infection with one serotype gives lifelong protection against that serotype, but only short-term protection against the others. A later infection with a different serotype can increase the risk of severe dengue.

## 3. Risk Factors
- Living in or travelling to areas where dengue is present, especially in urban and semi-urban areas in the tropics and subtropics
- Mosquito breeding near homes: stagnant water in containers, coolers, tyres, flower pots, water tanks, and construction sites
- Crowded housing and poor water storage or waste management
- Not using protection against mosquito bites
- Previous dengue infection (a second infection with a different serotype raises the risk of severe disease)
- Monsoon and post-monsoon seasons, when mosquito numbers often rise
- Climate and environmental factors such as rainfall, humidity and temperature

## 4. Transmission
- **Main route:** The bite of an infected female *Aedes* mosquito, mainly *Aedes aegypti* and, to a lesser extent, *Aedes albopictus*.
- **Biting time:** These mosquitoes bite mainly during the **daytime**, with peaks in early morning and late afternoon, which differs from malaria-carrying mosquitoes.
- **Breeding:** They breed in small collections of clean, stagnant water in and around homes.
- **Cycle:** A mosquito becomes infected when it bites a person who has the virus in their blood. After a short incubation period in the mosquito, it can pass the virus on when it bites other people.
- **Incubation period in humans:** Symptoms typically start about 4 to 10 days after the bite.
- **Person-to-person:** Dengue does not spread through casual contact, coughing or touching. Rarely, it can pass from a pregnant woman to her baby, and in very rare cases through blood transfusion or organ transplant.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
Many infections are mild or silent. When symptoms occur, they commonly last 2 to 7 days and may include:
- Sudden high fever
- Severe headache
- Pain behind the eyes
- Muscle and joint pain (hence the nickname "breakbone fever")
- Nausea and vomiting
- Skin rash
- Tiredness and weakness
- Swollen glands

### Less Common Symptoms
- Mild bleeding such as nosebleeds or bleeding gums
- Easy bruising
- Loss of appetite
- Sore throat or mild cough
- Abdominal discomfort
- Low platelet count (found on a blood test)
- In young children: irritability, fever and rash that can be mistaken for other illnesses

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Fever of 2 days or more in an area where dengue is present
- High fever with severe headache, body pain, or pain behind the eyes
- Fever with rash
- Not drinking or eating enough, or signs of dehydration (very dry mouth, little urine, dizziness)
- Ongoing vomiting
- Fever in infants, young children, pregnant women or older adults
- Fever after returning from an area with dengue

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Dangerous complications usually appear around the time the **fever begins to fall (often day 3 to 7 of illness)**. This is the "critical phase". Feeling better as the fever drops does not always mean recovery. Seek **urgent medical care immediately** (go to the nearest hospital) if any of these occur:
- Severe, continuous abdominal pain or tenderness
- Persistent vomiting (unable to keep fluids down)
- Bleeding from the nose, gums, or under the skin; vomiting blood; blood in stool; black stools; heavy menstrual bleeding
- Rapid breathing or difficulty breathing
- Extreme tiredness, restlessness, irritability or drowsiness
- Cold, clammy or pale skin, cold hands and feet
- Very little or no urine
- Fainting, confusion or severe weakness
- Rapid weak pulse or very low blood pressure
- Fluid build-up (swelling of the abdomen, or breathlessness)

## 7. Diagnosis
Dengue is diagnosed by a healthcare professional using the person's symptoms, travel and exposure history, and laboratory tests.
- **NS1 antigen test:** Detects a dengue virus protein, most useful in the **first days** of illness (typically within the first 5 or so days).
- **RT-PCR (molecular) test:** Detects the virus's genetic material in the early phase and can identify the serotype.
- **Serology (IgM and IgG antibody tests):** Detect the body's immune response, which appears later in the illness. IgM indicates recent infection; results can be hard to interpret after prior dengue or related flavivirus infection.
- **Complete blood count:** Doctors monitor **platelet count** and **haematocrit** (concentration of red cells). A falling platelet count and rising haematocrit can signal worsening illness.
- **Other tests:** Liver tests, kidney function and imaging may be done in severe cases.

Testing and monitoring should be guided by a doctor. A single test result must be interpreted in the context of how many days the person has been ill.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions are made by a doctor based on the person's condition, age, and stage of illness. Do not self-medicate.*

- **No specific antiviral treatment exists.** Care is supportive.
- **Rest and fluids:** Drinking plenty of fluids (such as oral rehydration solution, water, soups, coconut water, fruit juice) helps prevent dehydration. Doctors will advise how much is suitable for each person.
- **Fever and pain relief:** Doctors commonly advise **paracetamol (acetaminophen)** for fever and pain, taken only as directed by a healthcare professional.
- **Avoid certain painkillers:** **Aspirin and NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, diclofenac)** are generally **not recommended** in suspected dengue, because they can increase the risk of bleeding. Check with a doctor before taking any medicine.
- **Medical monitoring:** People with suspected dengue should be seen by a doctor, who may advise regular blood tests (platelets, haematocrit) and follow-up, particularly around the time fever falls.
- **Hospital care:** People with warning signs, severe dengue, or special risks (infants, pregnancy, older adults, other illness) need hospital monitoring. Severe dengue is treated with careful intravenous fluids, and sometimes blood products or other supportive care.
- **Platelet counts:** A low platelet count alone does not always mean a transfusion is needed; doctors decide based on bleeding and the overall clinical picture.
- **Protect others during illness:** While feverish, sleep under a mosquito net or in a screened room, so mosquitoes do not bite the patient and spread the virus to others.
- **Recovery:** Many people recover in about one to two weeks, though tiredness may last longer.
- **In India,** diagnosis and care for dengue are available at government health facilities, and testing and treatment follow national guidelines. Ask at the nearest health centre or hospital.

## 9. Prevention
There is no vaccine that works as a simple first line of protection for everyone, so **preventing mosquito bites and controlling mosquito breeding is the most important protection.**
- **Remove standing water** around the home: empty and scrub water containers, coolers, flower vases, plant saucers, buckets and old tyres at least once a week; cover water tanks and storage containers tightly.
- **Prevent bites:** Use mosquito repellents (as recommended by health authorities), wear long sleeves and trousers, and use window and door screens, coils or vaporisers, and bed nets (useful for people resting by day, such as infants and those who are ill).
- **Community action:** Participate in local clean-up and fogging or larval control campaigns organised by health authorities, and keep surroundings clean.
- **Early care:** Seek medical care for fever early, and avoid delays.
- **Vaccines:** Dengue vaccines exist but are limited in use. WHO has recommended the vaccine TAK-003 (Qdenga) for children aged 6 to 16 in settings with high dengue transmission. Another vaccine, Dengvaxia, is only for people with confirmed prior dengue infection. Availability and approval differ by country, so ask a doctor or local health authorities about what is available. Vaccination does not replace mosquito control.
- **Travellers:** Use bite protection during the day, and see a doctor if fever develops during or after travel to a dengue area.

## 10. Complications
- **Severe dengue:** Plasma leakage from blood vessels leading to shock, severe bleeding or serious organ impairment.
- **Dengue shock syndrome:** Dangerously low blood pressure and poor circulation.
- **Severe bleeding**, including from the digestive tract.
- **Organ involvement:** Liver, heart, kidneys and brain can be affected in rare cases.
- **Fluid build-up** in the chest or abdomen that causes breathing difficulty.
- **Dehydration** from fever, vomiting or poor intake.
- **Prolonged fatigue, weakness or low mood** after recovery in some people.
- **Complications in pregnancy:** Possible premature birth, low birth weight, or infection in the newborn.
- **Death** can occur if severe dengue is not recognised and managed in time. With early detection and good care, the risk is greatly reduced.

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Infants and young children
- Pregnant women
- Older adults
- People who have had dengue before (second infection with a different serotype)
- People with chronic conditions such as diabetes, high blood pressure, heart, kidney or liver disease, asthma, or obesity
- People with blood disorders or who are on blood-thinning medicines
- People living in crowded urban areas with poor water storage or sanitation
- People living in or travelling to dengue-affected regions
- People with limited access to healthcare, who may delay seeking care

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Fever lasting more than 1 to 2 days in a dengue-affected area, especially with headache, body pain, pain behind the eyes or rash
- Fever in a child, pregnant woman or older adult
- Recent travel to a dengue-affected area and fever
- Any warning signs (severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, breathlessness, drowsiness or restlessness, cold clammy skin, reduced urine). **Go to hospital immediately.**
- Fever that has settled but the person feels worse or very weak
- Before taking any painkiller for fever in suspected dengue, to avoid medicines that raise bleeding risk
- Any concern about the person not being able to drink enough fluids

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Dengue spreads from person to person."** It spreads through mosquito bites, not casual contact.
2. **"Dengue mosquitoes bite only at night."** *Aedes* mosquitoes bite mainly during the day.
3. **"Dengue mosquitoes breed in dirty drains."** They prefer small collections of clean stagnant water in and around homes.
4. **"Papaya leaf juice or other home remedies cure dengue."** No home remedy has been proven to cure dengue. Rely on medical care, and discuss any supplements with a doctor.
5. **"Low platelets always mean a platelet transfusion is needed."** Doctors decide on transfusion based on bleeding and overall condition, not the platelet number alone.
6. **"Once the fever goes, the danger is over."** The critical phase often begins as the fever falls, so careful monitoring is still needed.
7. **"I can take aspirin or ibuprofen for dengue fever."** These are generally avoided in dengue because of bleeding risk; ask a doctor.
8. **"You can only get dengue once."** There are four serotypes; you can be infected more than once.
9. **"All dengue is severe."** Most infections are mild or without symptoms, but severe dengue can occur and needs urgent care.
10. **"Dengue only occurs in the monsoon."** Cases peak in and after the rainy season, but dengue can occur at other times of the year too.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is dengue?**
Dengue is a viral illness spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes. It causes fever and body aches and can sometimes become severe.

**Q2. How does dengue spread?**
Through the bite of an infected *Aedes* mosquito, mainly *Aedes aegypti*. It does not spread directly from person to person through touch or coughing.

**Q3. What are the symptoms of dengue?**
Sudden high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, nausea, vomiting, rash and tiredness. Many people have mild or no symptoms.

**Q4. How long after a mosquito bite do symptoms appear?**
Usually 4 to 10 days after the bite.

**Q5. What are the warning signs of severe dengue?**
Severe belly pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, rapid breathing, extreme tiredness or restlessness, cold clammy skin, and very little urine. These often appear when the fever is falling. Go to hospital immediately.

**Q6. How is dengue diagnosed?**
By a doctor's assessment and blood tests such as NS1 antigen, RT-PCR and antibody tests, together with blood counts that track platelets and haematocrit.

**Q7. Is there a specific medicine to treat dengue?**
No specific antiviral exists. Treatment is supportive: rest, fluids, fever control as advised by a doctor, and monitoring, with hospital care if the illness becomes severe.

**Q8. Which medicines should be avoided in dengue?**
Aspirin and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen are generally avoided because they can raise bleeding risk. Always check with a doctor before taking any medicine.

**Q9. How much fluid should I drink during dengue?**
Fluids are important to prevent dehydration, but the right amount differs from person to person. A doctor will advise based on the person's age, condition and test results.

**Q10. Does a low platelet count mean the illness is severe?**
Platelet counts often fall in dengue, but doctors look at the whole picture, including bleeding, symptoms and other test results.

**Q11. How can I prevent dengue?**
Remove standing water around your home weekly, cover water containers, use repellents, screens and nets, wear protective clothing, and support community mosquito control.

**Q12. Is there a vaccine for dengue?**
There are dengue vaccines, but their use is limited. WHO recommends one (TAK-003) for children aged 6 to 16 in high-transmission areas, and another (Dengvaxia) only for people with confirmed past infection. Availability varies by country, so ask your doctor or health authority. Mosquito control remains essential.

**Q13. Can I get dengue more than once?**
Yes. There are four serotypes, and you can be infected by each of them. A second infection with a different serotype can raise the risk of severe disease.

**Q14. Can dengue affect pregnant women and children more seriously?**
Yes. Pregnant women, infants and young children are at higher risk of complications and need prompt medical assessment.

**Q15. When can I return to normal activities after dengue?**
Recovery varies. Tiredness can last for days or weeks. A doctor should advise when it is safe to return to work, school or exercise, and when fever and other symptoms have settled.
