# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Influenza (flu)
Medical Name: Influenza (caused by influenza viruses, mainly types A and B)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (viral); acute respiratory illness; airborne / droplet-spread
Also Known As: Flu, seasonal flu, seasonal influenza; named strains include "H1N1" (sometimes called "swine flu" after the 2009 pandemic) and "H3N2"

## 1. Overview
Influenza ("the flu") is a contagious respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses. It infects the nose, throat and sometimes the lungs. Flu is **not the same as the common cold**. It usually comes on suddenly and tends to cause a more intense illness, with high fever, body aches and tiredness.

Most people recover on their own within about a week, though cough and tiredness can last longer. However, flu can cause **severe illness and death**, especially in young children, older adults, pregnant women, and people with chronic health conditions. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), seasonal influenza epidemics occur every year around the world, and the flu is an important cause of hospitalisation and death. See WHO's current pages for the latest figures.

Flu can be prevented with **yearly vaccination** and good hygiene, and treated with antiviral medicines in people at higher risk or with severe illness. In India and other tropical and subtropical regions, flu can occur throughout the year, with peaks often around the rainy season and winter months.

## 2. Causes
- Flu is caused by **influenza viruses**. There are four types: A, B, C and D.
- **Influenza A and B** cause seasonal epidemics in people. Influenza A viruses are further divided into subtypes based on two surface proteins, **H (haemagglutinin) and N (neuraminidase)**, such as **H1N1** and **H3N2**. Influenza B viruses are grouped into lineages.
- Influenza C usually causes mild illness. Influenza D mainly affects cattle and is not known to cause illness in people.
- Flu viruses **change constantly** (a process called antigenic drift), which is why people can get flu repeatedly and why vaccines are updated regularly.
- Occasionally, a new influenza A virus (often from animals such as birds or pigs) can infect people and spread widely, causing a **pandemic**. Avian (bird) influenza viruses such as H5N1 can infect people but do not normally spread easily between humans.

## 3. Risk Factors
**Risk of catching flu:**
- Close contact with someone who has flu
- Crowded or enclosed settings, such as schools, workplaces, public transport, care homes and hospitals
- Not being vaccinated against flu in the current season
- Seasonal peaks (rainy and winter periods in many regions)
- Poor hand and respiratory hygiene

**Risk of severe illness:**
- Age: young children (especially under 5 years, and highest risk under 2) and older adults (65 years and above)
- Pregnancy
- Chronic conditions: asthma, chronic lung disease, heart disease, diabetes, chronic kidney or liver disease, neurological conditions
- Weakened immune system (for example, HIV, cancer treatment, organ transplant, long-term steroid use)
- Obesity
- Residents of long-term care facilities
- Not being vaccinated

## 4. Transmission
- **Main routes:** Through droplets and aerosols released when an infected person coughs, sneezes, talks or breathes. Others can breathe these in or have them land on their eyes, nose or mouth.
- **Contact:** Touching surfaces or objects with flu virus on them and then touching the mouth, nose or eyes can also spread flu, though this is thought to be less common than spread through the air.
- **Incubation period:** Symptoms usually start about 2 days after infection, with a range of about 1 to 4 days.
- **Infectious period:** People can spread flu from about **1 day before** symptoms begin and for around **5 to 7 days after** symptoms start. Young children and people with weakened immunity may remain infectious for longer.
- **Asymptomatic spread:** Some people are infected and have mild or no symptoms, yet can still spread the virus.
- **Seasonality:** In temperate regions flu peaks in winter. In tropical and subtropical regions, such as India, flu can occur year-round with peaks that vary by area.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
Flu symptoms typically come on suddenly:
- Fever (often high) and chills
- Cough (usually dry)
- Sore throat
- Muscle and body aches
- Headache
- Tiredness and weakness (can be intense)
- Runny or blocked nose
- Sweating

### Less Common Symptoms
- Loss of appetite
- Nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea (more common in children than adults)
- Eye redness or watering
- Chest discomfort
- Dizziness
- Ear pain in children
- Cough and tiredness that continue for a couple of weeks after other symptoms have eased
- Some people have no fever, particularly older adults

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Fever that lasts more than 3 to 4 days or returns after improving
- Symptoms in people at higher risk (young children, older adults, pregnant women, people with chronic conditions or weak immunity)
- Cough that becomes worse, producing coloured phlegm, or with chest pain
- Shortness of breath or wheezing
- Symptoms that improve and then suddenly worsen
- Dehydration (little urine, very dry mouth, dizziness)
- Ear pain or sinus pain that is severe or lasting
- Worsening of existing conditions such as asthma, diabetes or heart disease

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital or call local emergency services) if someone with flu or suspected flu has:
- Difficulty breathing, fast breathing or shortness of breath
- Persistent pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen
- Sudden dizziness, confusion or inability to wake up or stay awake
- Seizures
- Severe or persistent vomiting, or inability to drink fluids
- Bluish or grey lips, face or nails
- Not urinating, or signs of severe dehydration
- Fever or cough that improves but then returns or gets worse
- Severe muscle pain or weakness
- Worsening of chronic medical conditions

**In children, also seek urgent care for:**
- Fast breathing or trouble breathing
- Bluish skin colour
- Not drinking enough fluids
- Not waking up or not interacting
- Being so irritable that the child does not want to be held
- Fever with a rash
- Infants under 3 months with fever

## 7. Diagnosis
Flu is often diagnosed by a doctor based on symptoms and the time of year and local flu activity. Because many respiratory viruses cause similar symptoms (including COVID-19), testing may be used, especially for people at higher risk, with severe illness, or when the result would change treatment.
- **Rapid influenza diagnostic tests:** Quick tests on nose or throat swabs that give results within minutes. They are less sensitive than molecular tests, so a negative result does not completely rule out flu.
- **Molecular tests (RT-PCR and other nucleic acid amplification tests):** More accurate; can identify influenza A or B and sometimes the subtype.
- **Other tests:** Chest X-ray, blood tests and oxygen level checks may be used to assess severity or complications such as pneumonia.
- **Surveillance:** Health authorities test samples to monitor which flu strains are circulating, to guide vaccine composition and public health measures.
- Because flu and COVID-19 can look alike, a doctor may advise testing for both.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions, including whether to use antiviral medicines, are made by a doctor based on the person's age, risk factors, and how long they have been ill. Do not self-medicate.*

- **Most healthy people recover at home** with rest, plenty of fluids, and symptom relief.
- **Rest and fluids:** Rest, stay home, and drink enough fluids to prevent dehydration.
- **Fever and pain relief:** Medicines for fever and aches can be used as advised by a healthcare professional or according to the product label.
- **Avoid aspirin in children and teenagers:** Aspirin should not be given to children and teenagers with flu or flu-like illness because of the risk of **Reye's syndrome**, a rare but serious condition. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before giving any medicine to a child.
- **Antiviral medicines:** Prescription antivirals (examples include oseltamivir, zanamivir, baloxavir and peramivir) can reduce the severity and duration of flu, and are most effective when started **early, ideally within the first 48 hours** of symptoms. WHO and national guidelines recommend them for people with severe illness and for those at higher risk of complications; a doctor decides who needs them. They can be considered even after 48 hours in severe or high-risk cases.
- **Antibiotics:** Antibiotics do not work against flu viruses. A doctor may prescribe them only if a secondary bacterial infection (such as bacterial pneumonia) develops.
- **Hospital care:** Severe flu may require oxygen, intravenous fluids, antiviral medicines, and treatment of complications, sometimes in intensive care.
- **Stay home while unwell:** Stay home until fever has resolved and you feel better, in line with local health guidance, to avoid spreading flu to others. Cover coughs and sneezes and wash hands frequently.
- **Recovery:** Fever usually settles in a few days, but tiredness and cough can last for a couple of weeks.
- **In India,** flu testing and treatment are available at government and private health facilities. Seek care at your nearest health centre or hospital if you are at higher risk or have warning signs.

## 9. Prevention
- **Annual flu vaccination:** The best way to prevent flu and its severe outcomes. Because flu viruses change, vaccine composition is reviewed and updated regularly, and annual vaccination is recommended. WHO recommends vaccination especially for high-risk groups: pregnant women, children aged 6 months to 5 years, older adults, people with chronic medical conditions, and health workers. Ask your doctor or local health authority about vaccine availability, timing and suitability. Vaccine protection is not complete, but it reduces the risk of illness and of severe disease. In regions with year-round flu, timing recommendations vary.
- **Hand hygiene:** Wash hands often with soap and water, or use an alcohol-based hand rub.
- **Respiratory etiquette:** Cover your mouth and nose with a tissue or your elbow when coughing or sneezing, dispose of tissues safely, and wash hands afterwards.
- **Stay home when sick,** and avoid close contact with others while infectious.
- **Avoid touching your face** (eyes, nose, mouth) with unwashed hands.
- **Masks and ventilation:** Wearing a well-fitting mask when ill or when caring for someone with flu, and improving ventilation in crowded indoor places, can help reduce spread.
- **Clean frequently touched surfaces** regularly.
- **Avoid close contact** with people who are sick, especially if you are at higher risk.
- **Healthy habits:** Adequate sleep, nutrition and physical activity support general health.
- **Antiviral prevention:** In some situations, doctors may prescribe antivirals to prevent flu in high-risk people exposed to the virus. This is a medical decision.

## 10. Complications
- **Pneumonia** (viral or secondary bacterial)
- **Bronchitis** and worsening of asthma or chronic obstructive lung disease
- **Sinus and ear infections**
- **Dehydration**
- **Heart complications:** Inflammation of the heart muscle (myocarditis), and increased risk of heart attack in people with heart disease
- **Neurological complications:** Encephalitis and, rarely, Guillain-Barré syndrome
- **Muscle inflammation** (myositis), particularly in children
- **Sepsis and multi-organ failure** in severe cases
- **Worsening of chronic conditions** such as diabetes, heart failure, kidney disease
- **Reye's syndrome** in children given aspirin
- **Pregnancy complications:** Risk of severe illness for the mother, and risks to the baby such as preterm birth
- **Death:** Can occur, particularly among older adults, young children, and people with other medical conditions

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Children under 5 years (especially under 2)
- Adults aged 65 years and older
- Pregnant women and women up to two weeks after delivery
- People with chronic conditions: asthma, chronic lung disease, heart disease, diabetes, chronic kidney or liver disease, neurological or neuromuscular conditions
- People with weakened immunity (for example, HIV, cancer treatment, transplant recipients, long-term steroid use)
- People with obesity
- Residents of long-term care facilities and nursing homes
- Children and adolescents on long-term aspirin therapy
- Health workers and caregivers (higher exposure)
- People living in crowded settings
- People with limited access to healthcare or vaccination

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- You or a family member are in a **high-risk group** and develop flu-like symptoms. Contact a doctor early, since antiviral treatment works best when started soon after symptoms begin.
- Fever lasting more than 3 to 4 days, or returning after improving
- Cough that worsens, produces coloured phlegm, or comes with chest pain
- Shortness of breath, wheezing, or chest tightness
- Symptoms that get better and then suddenly worsen
- Signs of dehydration, or inability to drink fluids
- A baby, young child, pregnant woman or older adult with fever or flu-like symptoms
- A chronic condition worsens during illness
- Any warning sign in Section 6 (**go to hospital immediately**)
- Before taking any antiviral or antibiotic medicine, or giving medicine to a child
- To ask about annual flu vaccination

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Flu is just a bad cold."** Flu is usually more severe than a cold and can cause serious complications and death.
2. **"The flu vaccine gives you the flu."** Flu vaccines (injected, inactivated) cannot cause flu. Mild side effects such as a sore arm or low fever can happen and pass quickly.
3. **"Healthy people don't need to worry about flu."** Healthy people can also become seriously ill, and they can spread the virus to those at higher risk.
4. **"Antibiotics treat the flu."** Antibiotics do not work against viruses. They are used only for secondary bacterial infections.
5. **"I got vaccinated last year, so I'm covered."** Flu viruses change, and immunity fades, so annual vaccination is recommended.
6. **"The flu is the same as a stomach bug."** "Stomach flu" is usually caused by other viruses (such as norovirus). True influenza is mainly a respiratory illness, though children may have vomiting or diarrhoea.
7. **"You can't spread flu if you have no symptoms."** People can spread flu before symptoms start and, sometimes, without ever feeling unwell.
8. **"Cold weather or being wet causes flu."** Flu is caused by a virus. Cold weather may encourage indoor crowding and favour virus spread, but the virus is needed to cause infection.
9. **"If I'm in a tropical country, there is no flu season."** Flu occurs year-round in many tropical regions, including India, often with seasonal peaks.
10. **"Antivirals are only useful in the first hour."** Antivirals work best when started early (ideally within 48 hours), but they can still help later in severe or high-risk cases, as a doctor decides.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is influenza (flu)?**
Flu is a contagious respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses. It causes sudden fever, cough, body aches and tiredness, and can sometimes lead to serious complications.

**Q2. How is the flu different from the common cold?**
Flu usually starts suddenly and is more severe, with high fever, body aches and tiredness. Colds are usually milder, with mainly a runny nose and sore throat.

**Q3. How does the flu spread?**
Mainly through droplets and tiny airborne particles when an infected person coughs, sneezes, talks or breathes. It can also spread by touching contaminated surfaces and then your face.

**Q4. How long is a person with flu contagious?**
From about a day before symptoms start to roughly 5 to 7 days after they begin. Young children and people with weakened immunity may be contagious for longer.

**Q5. What are the symptoms of flu?**
Sudden fever, chills, cough, sore throat, muscle aches, headache, tiredness and a runny or blocked nose.

**Q6. How long does flu last?**
Most people feel better within about a week, though cough and tiredness can last for two weeks or more.

**Q7. How is flu diagnosed?**
Often by symptoms and local flu activity, and sometimes by rapid tests or molecular (PCR) tests on a nose or throat swab. Flu and COVID-19 can look alike, so doctors may test for both.

**Q8. How is flu treated?**
Most people recover with rest, fluids and symptom relief. Prescription antiviral medicines can help people who are very ill or at higher risk, especially if started early. Antibiotics do not work against flu. Ask a doctor, and do not self-medicate.

**Q9. Can children take aspirin for flu?**
No. Aspirin should not be given to children and teenagers with flu because of the risk of Reye's syndrome. Always ask a doctor or pharmacist before giving a child any medicine.

**Q10. Who should get the flu vaccine?**
WHO recommends annual vaccination especially for pregnant women, young children (6 months to 5 years), older adults, people with chronic conditions, and health workers. A doctor or local health authority can advise who should be vaccinated and when.

**Q11. Why do I need a flu vaccine every year?**
Flu viruses change over time, so vaccines are updated to match circulating strains, and protection from vaccination fades.

**Q12. Can the flu vaccine give me the flu?**
No. Injected flu vaccines contain inactivated (killed) virus or parts of it and cannot cause flu. Mild, short-lived side effects can occur.

**Q13. How can I stop spreading flu to others?**
Stay home while sick, cover coughs and sneezes, wash your hands often, wear a mask around others if you must be near them, and keep rooms well ventilated.

**Q14. What is the difference between seasonal flu and "swine flu" or "bird flu"?**
Seasonal flu viruses (such as H1N1 and H3N2) circulate in people every year. "Swine flu" (H1N1) refers to a virus that caused the 2009 pandemic and now circulates as a seasonal virus. "Bird flu" (such as H5N1) mainly infects birds, with occasional human cases, and does not usually spread easily between people.

**Q15. Where can I get flu testing, treatment and vaccination in India?**
At government health facilities such as primary health centres and hospitals, and at private facilities. Ask at your nearest health centre or a local health worker about testing, treatment and the availability of flu vaccines.
