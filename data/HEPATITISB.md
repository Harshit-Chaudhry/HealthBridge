# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Hepatitis B
Medical Name: Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection
Disease Category: Infectious disease (viral); liver infection; blood-borne; vaccine-preventable
Also Known As: Hep B, HBV infection, serum hepatitis (older term), "Hepatitis B" (acute or chronic forms)

## 1. Overview
Hepatitis B is a viral infection that attacks the **liver**. It is caused by the **hepatitis B virus (HBV)**. It can cause both **acute** (short-term) illness and **chronic** (long-term) infection.

Many people, especially young children, have no symptoms when first infected. Most healthy adults clear the virus on their own. However, in some people the virus stays in the body for life (**chronic hepatitis B**), which can lead to serious liver problems such as **cirrhosis (scarring) and liver cancer** many years later.

Hepatitis B is **preventable with a safe and effective vaccine**. Chronic infection cannot usually be cured, but **medicines can control the virus and reduce the risk of liver damage**, and regular monitoring is important. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), hepatitis B is a major global health problem, with a large number of people living with chronic infection, many of whom do not know they are infected. See WHO's current pages for the latest figures.

Hepatitis B is different from hepatitis A, C, D and E, which are caused by different viruses.

## 2. Causes
- Hepatitis B is caused by the **hepatitis B virus (HBV)**, a DNA virus that infects liver cells.
- The virus is found in the blood and certain body fluids of infected people.
- The virus is **very infectious** (far more than HIV) and can survive outside the body on surfaces for at least 7 days.
- The body's immune response to the virus causes liver inflammation. In some people the immune system cannot clear the virus, leading to chronic infection.

## 3. Risk Factors
**Risk of infection:**
- Being born to a mother with hepatitis B (the risk is highest at birth)
- Not being vaccinated
- Sexual contact with an infected partner without protection
- Sharing needles, syringes or other injecting equipment
- Unsafe medical, dental or injection practices (reuse of needles or unsterilised equipment)
- Unsafe tattooing, piercing or acupuncture with non-sterile equipment
- Needle-stick and sharps injuries (health workers)
- Sharing personal items that may have blood on them, such as razors, toothbrushes or nail clippers
- Living with someone who has chronic hepatitis B
- Receiving blood or blood products that have not been screened (risk is very low where blood is screened)
- People on haemodialysis
- Travelling to or living in regions where hepatitis B is common
- Having HIV or hepatitis C (shared risk factors)

**Risk of chronic infection:**
- Infection at a young age: the younger the age at infection, the higher the risk of chronic infection (infants and young children are at particularly high risk)
- Weakened immune system

## 4. Transmission
- **Blood and body fluids:** HBV spreads through contact with the **blood, semen, vaginal fluids or other body fluids** of an infected person.
- **Mother to child:** At birth (perinatal transmission), which is a major route in regions where hepatitis B is common. Newborns can be protected with timely vaccination.
- **Early childhood:** From child to child in household contact in some settings.
- **Sexual contact:** Unprotected sex with an infected partner.
- **Unsafe injections and medical procedures:** Reuse of needles, syringes or equipment that has not been properly sterilised.
- **Sharing needles** for drug use.
- **Needle-stick injuries** and contact with infected blood on broken skin or mucous membranes.
- **Incubation period:** Usually about 30 to 180 days after exposure (average about 75 days).
- **Not spread by:** Casual contact such as hugging, shaking hands, kissing, sharing food, utensils or water, sneezing or coughing, using the same toilet, or mosquito bites. **Breastfeeding is not considered a route of transmission** in babies who receive proper vaccination and care, according to WHO and CDC guidance.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
Many people, particularly infants, children and some adults, have **no symptoms** at first. When symptoms of **acute hepatitis B** occur, they usually appear about 1 to 4 months after infection and may include:
- Fatigue and weakness
- Loss of appetite
- Nausea and vomiting
- Abdominal pain, especially in the upper right area (over the liver)
- **Jaundice**: yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes
- Dark (tea-coloured) urine
- Pale or clay-coloured stools

### Less Common Symptoms
- Low-grade fever
- Joint pain
- Rash or itching
- Muscle aches
- General feeling of being unwell
- Symptoms of **chronic hepatitis B** are often absent for many years. When liver damage progresses, symptoms may include tiredness, swelling of the abdomen or legs, easy bruising or bleeding, and confusion.

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Yellowing of the skin or eyes
- Dark urine or pale stools
- Persistent tiredness, nausea, vomiting or abdominal pain
- Loss of appetite and unexplained weight loss
- Known exposure to hepatitis B (for example, through a needle-stick injury or sexual contact with an infected person)
- A positive hepatitis B test, or being a close contact or family member of someone with hepatitis B
- A pregnant woman who has, or may have, hepatitis B

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital or call local emergency services) if a person with known or suspected hepatitis B has:
- Severe or worsening jaundice
- Persistent vomiting and inability to keep fluids or medicines down
- Confusion, severe drowsiness, unusual behaviour, personality change, difficulty waking, or loss of consciousness (possible acute liver failure or hepatic encephalopathy)
- Vomiting blood or passing black or bloody stools (possible bleeding from the digestive tract in advanced liver disease)
- Severe abdominal pain or a rapidly swelling abdomen
- Easy or unexplained bruising or bleeding
- Swelling of the abdomen or legs along with breathing difficulty
- Fever with abdominal swelling or pain (possible infection of fluid in the abdomen)

## 7. Diagnosis
Hepatitis B cannot be diagnosed by symptoms alone and requires blood tests.
- **HBsAg (hepatitis B surface antigen):** Shows current infection. If positive for **more than 6 months**, this indicates chronic hepatitis B.
- **Anti-HBs (antibody to surface antigen):** Indicates immunity, from vaccination or from recovery from past infection.
- **Anti-HBc (antibody to core antigen):** Indicates exposure to the virus at some point. **IgM anti-HBc** suggests recent (acute) infection.
- **HBeAg and anti-HBe:** Give information about viral activity and infectiousness.
- **HBV DNA (viral load):** Measures the amount of virus in the blood, used to guide treatment decisions and monitor treatment.
- **Liver function tests (such as ALT and AST):** Show liver inflammation.
- **Liver assessment:** Ultrasound, and tests of liver stiffness (such as elastography or blood-based scores), and sometimes liver biopsy, to check for liver scarring (fibrosis or cirrhosis).
- **Screening for liver cancer:** People with chronic hepatitis B, especially those with cirrhosis or certain risk factors, may need regular ultrasound and blood tests (such as alpha-fetoprotein), as advised by a doctor.
- **Who should be tested:** WHO and national guidelines recommend testing for people at higher risk, such as pregnant women, health workers, people with HIV, household and sexual contacts of infected people, and people from areas where hepatitis B is common. Testing for **HIV and hepatitis C** is often recommended as well.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions are made by a doctor (often a specialist in liver disease), based on blood tests, liver health and other factors. Do not self-medicate.*

- **Acute hepatitis B:** Usually treated with supportive care: rest, good nutrition, fluids, and avoiding alcohol and medicines that can harm the liver. Most adults recover fully. Severe acute hepatitis B or liver failure needs hospital care, and sometimes a liver transplant.
- **Chronic hepatitis B:** Cannot usually be completely cured, but **antiviral medicines can suppress the virus**, slow liver damage, reduce the risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer, and improve survival. WHO recommends oral antivirals such as **tenofovir** or **entecavir** for those who meet treatment criteria. Treatment is often long-term, sometimes lifelong, and should not be stopped without a doctor's advice.
- **Not everyone needs treatment right away:** Some people with chronic hepatitis B are monitored with regular blood tests and liver checks until they meet criteria for treatment.
- **Regular monitoring:** Ongoing blood tests (liver tests, viral load), imaging, and liver cancer screening as advised by a doctor.
- **Lifestyle:** **Avoid alcohol**, maintain a healthy weight, and eat a balanced diet. Ask a doctor before taking any medicines, herbal remedies or supplements, since some can harm the liver.
- **Vaccination for other types:** People with chronic hepatitis B are often advised to be vaccinated against hepatitis A.
- **Pregnancy:** Pregnant women with hepatitis B need specialist care. Medicines may be used in late pregnancy for some women to lower the risk of passing the virus to the baby, and the baby needs timely vaccination (and sometimes immunoglobulin) after birth.
- **Reactivation risk:** In people with past or chronic hepatitis B who receive chemotherapy, immune-suppressing medicines or certain other treatments, the virus can reactivate and cause serious liver disease. Tell all doctors about a history of hepatitis B before starting such treatments.
- **Support:** Counselling and support help reduce stigma and support long-term care.
- **In India,** the National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme provides diagnosis and treatment services at government facilities. Ask at your nearest health centre or hospital about availability.

## 9. Prevention
- **Vaccination:** The hepatitis B vaccine is **safe and highly effective** and is the main way to prevent infection. It is usually given as a **series of doses** (commonly 3 doses, depending on the schedule).
- **Birth dose:** WHO recommends that all newborns receive the first dose **within 24 hours of birth**, followed by additional doses, to prevent mother-to-child transmission. In India, the hepatitis B birth dose and further doses (as part of the pentavalent vaccine) are part of the national immunisation schedule. Check with a health worker for the current schedule.
- **Newborns of infected mothers:** Babies born to mothers with hepatitis B should get the vaccine promptly and, in some cases, hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG), as advised by a doctor.
- **Adults at risk:** Adults who have not been vaccinated, especially health workers, people with chronic liver disease, people with diabetes, household and sexual contacts of infected people, and people who inject drugs, should ask a doctor about vaccination.
- **Safe injections and medical care:** Use only sterile, single-use needles and syringes. Ensure medical, dental and tattoo or piercing equipment is properly sterilised.
- **Safe blood supply:** Blood should be screened.
- **Safer sex:** Use condoms, and limit the number of sexual partners.
- **Do not share** razors, toothbrushes, nail clippers or needles.
- **Harm reduction:** For people who inject drugs, use sterile equipment and access harm-reduction services.
- **Post-exposure care:** If you have been exposed to hepatitis B (such as through a needle-stick injury or sexual contact), contact a doctor immediately. Vaccination, with or without immunoglobulin, given soon after exposure can prevent infection in some people.
- **Testing and treatment:** Testing pregnant women, family members and other contacts, and linking people to care, helps prevent spread and complications.
- **Reduce stigma:** Hepatitis B does not spread through casual contact, so people with hepatitis B can safely live, study and work with others.

## 10. Complications
- **Chronic hepatitis B infection:** Lifelong infection in some people, particularly those infected as infants or young children
- **Cirrhosis:** Scarring of the liver that can cause liver failure
- **Liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma):** Risk is higher in chronic hepatitis B, even in some people without cirrhosis
- **Liver failure:** Acute (sudden) or chronic
- **Portal hypertension and its complications:** Fluid build-up in the abdomen (ascites), bleeding from swollen veins in the gullet (varices), and confusion (hepatic encephalopathy)
- **Kidney problems and other conditions** related to chronic infection (for example, certain immune-related conditions)
- **Co-infection:** Hepatitis D (delta) can infect people who already have hepatitis B and make disease more severe. Co-infection with HIV or hepatitis C can speed up liver damage.
- **Reactivation:** Virus can become active again in people with past infection if their immune system is suppressed
- **Mother-to-child transmission:** Infected infants have a high chance of developing chronic infection
- **Death:** WHO reports that hepatitis B causes a large number of deaths each year, mostly from cirrhosis and liver cancer. See WHO for current figures.

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Infants born to mothers with hepatitis B
- Unvaccinated infants, children and adults
- Household members and sexual partners of people with hepatitis B
- Health workers and others exposed to blood or sharps
- People who inject drugs
- People with multiple sexual partners
- People on haemodialysis
- People with HIV or hepatitis C
- People who receive frequent blood products or invasive procedures where infection control may be weak
- People who live in or have migrated from regions where hepatitis B is common
- People in institutional settings (such as prisons) and some other congregate settings
- People with chronic liver disease or diabetes (for vaccination priority)
- People starting chemotherapy or immune-suppressing treatment, who may be at risk of reactivation

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, pale stools, persistent tiredness, nausea, or abdominal pain
- Possible exposure to hepatitis B (needle-stick injury, sexual contact with an infected person, sharing needles). **Seek medical advice as soon as possible**, because prompt vaccination or immunoglobulin may prevent infection.
- You have never been tested or vaccinated and belong to a higher-risk group
- You are pregnant, or planning pregnancy, and want to be tested, or you have hepatitis B
- You have been diagnosed with hepatitis B. **Regular follow-up is important** even if you feel well.
- You have hepatitis B and are about to start chemotherapy or immune-suppressing medicines
- You are a close contact (household or sexual) of someone with hepatitis B
- Any warning sign in Section 6 (**go to hospital immediately**)
- Before taking new medicines, herbal products or supplements if you have hepatitis B
- To ask about hepatitis B vaccination for yourself or your child

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Hepatitis B spreads through food, water, or sharing meals."** Hepatitis B spreads through blood and body fluids, not through food, water, hugging, handshakes or sharing utensils.
2. **"If I feel well, I don't have hepatitis B."** Many people with hepatitis B have no symptoms for years while liver damage may be silently progressing. Testing is the only way to know.
3. **"Hepatitis B is the same as hepatitis A."** They are different viruses. Hepatitis A usually spreads through contaminated food and water and does not cause chronic infection.
4. **"Hepatitis B can't be prevented."** There is a safe and effective vaccine that prevents hepatitis B.
5. **"Hepatitis B is always lifelong."** Most healthy adults clear the virus within months. Chronic infection is more likely in infants and young children.
6. **"People with hepatitis B can't work, study or live with family."** Hepatitis B does not spread through everyday contact. People with hepatitis B can live normal lives and take part in school and work, with sensible precautions around blood.
7. **"Breastfeeding spreads hepatitis B."** Breastfeeding is not considered a route of transmission when the baby has received proper vaccination (and immunoglobulin when advised). Ask the doctor for advice.
8. **"Herbal or home remedies can cure hepatitis B."** There is no proven herbal cure. Some herbal products can even harm the liver. Rely on medical care.
9. **"Treatment is only needed when symptoms appear."** Decisions on treatment depend on blood tests and liver assessment, not just symptoms. Regular monitoring is important.
10. **"A vaccinated person can't get hepatitis B."** The vaccine is highly effective but protection can vary. Healthcare workers and others at risk may need antibody testing to confirm immunity. A doctor can advise.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is hepatitis B?**
Hepatitis B is a viral infection of the liver, caused by the hepatitis B virus. It can cause short-term illness or lifelong (chronic) infection that can lead to cirrhosis and liver cancer.

**Q2. How is hepatitis B spread?**
Through contact with the blood or certain body fluids of an infected person, such as at birth from mother to baby, through unprotected sex, sharing needles, unsafe injections or medical procedures, and needle-stick injuries.

**Q3. Can I get hepatitis B from casual contact?**
No. It does not spread through hugging, handshakes, sharing food or utensils, coughing, sneezing, or using the same toilet.

**Q4. What are the symptoms of hepatitis B?**
Many people have none. When symptoms occur, they can include tiredness, nausea, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, dark urine, pale stools, and yellowing of the skin and eyes.

**Q5. How long after exposure do symptoms appear?**
Usually about 1 to 4 months, though many people never develop noticeable symptoms.

**Q6. How is hepatitis B diagnosed?**
By blood tests such as HBsAg, antibody tests, and viral load (HBV DNA), along with liver function tests and sometimes imaging. Symptoms alone cannot confirm hepatitis B.

**Q7. What is chronic hepatitis B?**
It is infection that continues for more than 6 months. It is more likely when infection occurs in infancy or early childhood. It can silently damage the liver over many years.

**Q8. Can hepatitis B be cured?**
Acute infection in healthy adults usually clears on its own. Chronic infection generally cannot be completely cured, but antiviral medicines can control the virus and reduce the risk of serious liver disease. A specialist decides who needs treatment.

**Q9. What is the treatment for hepatitis B?**
Acute infection is treated with supportive care. Chronic infection may be treated with long-term antiviral medicines (such as tenofovir or entecavir) and regular monitoring. Do not self-medicate, and do not stop medicines without medical advice.

**Q10. How can I prevent hepatitis B?**
Get vaccinated, use sterile needles and equipment, practise safer sex, avoid sharing razors or toothbrushes, and make sure newborns receive the birth dose of vaccine.

**Q11. When should a baby get the hepatitis B vaccine?**
WHO recommends the first dose within 24 hours of birth, followed by further doses as per the national schedule. Babies born to mothers with hepatitis B may also need immunoglobulin, as advised by a doctor.

**Q12. Is the hepatitis B vaccine safe?**
Yes. It has been used for decades and has an excellent safety record. Mild side effects such as soreness at the injection site or mild fever may occur.

**Q13. Can a mother with hepatitis B breastfeed or safely give birth?**
Pregnant women with hepatitis B should be under specialist care. With timely vaccination of the baby (and immunoglobulin when advised), and sometimes antiviral medicine for the mother, the risk of passing hepatitis B to the baby can be greatly reduced. A doctor will advise on delivery and breastfeeding.

**Q14. Can I drink alcohol if I have hepatitis B?**
Alcohol can speed up liver damage, so people with hepatitis B are generally advised to avoid alcohol. Ask your doctor for advice.

**Q15. Where can I get hepatitis B testing, vaccination and treatment in India?**
At government and private health facilities, including primary health centres and hospitals. India's National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme provides testing and treatment services at government facilities. Ask at your nearest health centre or a local health worker.
