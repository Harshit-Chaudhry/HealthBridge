# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Malaria
Medical Name: Malaria (caused by *Plasmodium* parasites)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (parasitic); mosquito-borne (vector-borne)
Also Known As: Marsh fever, ague (historical terms); "Plasmodium infection"; types are named by parasite, for example falciparum malaria, vivax malaria

## 1. Overview
Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by microscopic parasites of the genus *Plasmodium*. People catch it through the bites of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. It is not passed on by casual contact between people.

Malaria is preventable and curable when diagnosed early and treated promptly. If treatment is delayed, especially with *P. falciparum* malaria, it can quickly become severe and fatal.

Malaria is mainly found in tropical and subtropical regions, with the highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa. It also occurs in parts of South and Southeast Asia (including India), Latin America, the Middle East and Oceania. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), malaria causes hundreds of thousands of deaths each year, most of them in young children in Africa. See the latest WHO World Malaria Report for current figures.

## 2. Causes
- Malaria is caused by *Plasmodium* parasites.
- Five species commonly infect humans: *P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae* and *P. knowlesi* (the last is mainly zoonotic, found in parts of Southeast Asia).
- *P. falciparum* is the most dangerous and causes most malaria deaths. *P. vivax* is widespread outside Africa, including in India, and can cause relapses.
- The parasite lives in the liver and red blood cells. When it multiplies in red blood cells it destroys them, which causes the symptoms.

## 3. Risk Factors
- Living in or travelling to areas where malaria is transmitted
- Being bitten by mosquitoes, especially between dusk and dawn, when *Anopheles* mosquitoes feed
- Not using protective measures (bed nets, repellents, protective clothing, preventive medicine for travellers)
- Housing that allows mosquitoes in (no screens, open eaves) and mosquito breeding sites nearby (stagnant water)
- Young age, pregnancy, HIV, or no previous exposure (see High-Risk Groups)
- Delay in diagnosis and treatment, which raises the risk of severe disease
- Lack of access to health services, and poverty
- Rarely, exposure through blood transfusion, organ transplant, shared needles, or from mother to baby during pregnancy or birth

## 4. Transmission
- **Main route:** The bite of an infected female *Anopheles* mosquito. The mosquito picks up parasites when it bites an infected person and passes them on when it bites someone else.
- **Timing:** Malaria-carrying mosquitoes mostly bite from dusk to dawn.
- **Other (less common) routes:** Congenital transmission (mother to unborn baby), blood transfusion, organ transplant, and sharing contaminated needles.
- **Not spread by:** Coughing, touching, shaking hands, sharing food or being near someone with malaria.
- **Environmental factors:** Rainfall, temperature and stagnant water influence mosquito numbers, so transmission can be seasonal.
- **Incubation:** Symptoms usually start about 10 to 15 days after an infected mosquito bite, but this varies by parasite species, and some infections can stay dormant for months (see Complications about relapse).

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
- Fever
- Chills and shivering, often with repeated cycles of chills, fever and sweating
- Sweating
- Headache
- Muscle aches and body pain
- Tiredness and weakness
- Nausea and vomiting

Early symptoms can resemble flu or other fevers (such as dengue), so malaria cannot be recognised by symptoms alone. A blood test is needed.

### Less Common Symptoms
- Diarrhoea and abdominal pain
- Cough
- Fast breathing or fast heartbeat
- Pale skin or yellowing of the eyes and skin (from anaemia or jaundice)
- Enlarged spleen
- In young children: poor feeding, irritability, drowsiness, fever without other obvious symptoms
- Repeated or periodic fevers in some types of malaria

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Any fever in a person living in or recently returned from an area with malaria
- Fever with chills and sweating that comes back in cycles
- Fever with headache, vomiting and body aches that does not settle
- Fever in a pregnant woman, a baby or a young child in a malaria area
- Fever up to a year (and sometimes longer) after travel to a malaria-risk area

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Severe malaria is a medical emergency. Seek urgent care immediately (go to the nearest hospital or call local emergency services) if a person with fever or suspected malaria has:
- Confusion, extreme drowsiness, difficulty waking up, or loss of consciousness
- Seizures (convulsions)
- Difficulty breathing or very fast breathing
- Severe weakness (unable to sit, stand or walk)
- Persistent vomiting, or unable to keep fluids or medicine down
- Very pale skin or yellow eyes and skin (severe anaemia or jaundice)
- Dark or very little urine, or dark red or brown urine
- Abnormal bleeding
- Very low blood sugar signs such as sweating, shakiness or fainting, especially in children

## 7. Diagnosis
Malaria is confirmed by laboratory testing. WHO recommends parasitological confirmation (a test showing the parasite) before treatment wherever possible.
- **Microscopy of blood smears:** A drop of blood is examined under a microscope to find malaria parasites and identify the species. This is the traditional standard.
- **Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs):** Quick finger-prick tests detecting malaria proteins (antigens). Results come within about 15 to 20 minutes and are widely used where microscopy is not available.
- **Molecular tests (PCR):** Used in some laboratories to identify species or low-level infection.
- **Additional tests:** Blood counts, blood sugar, kidney and liver tests, and others, when a doctor suspects severe malaria.
- **G6PD test:** Used before certain medicines for *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* are given, because these medicines can be harmful to people with G6PD deficiency (an inherited enzyme condition).

A single negative test does not always rule out malaria if symptoms continue. Doctors may repeat testing.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. The right medicines, doses and duration are decided only by a doctor or the national malaria programme, based on the parasite species, severity, age, pregnancy, local drug resistance and other factors. Do not self-medicate.*

- **Malaria is curable** with prompt, appropriate antimalarial treatment.
- **Uncomplicated *P. falciparum* malaria:** WHO recommends artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT), which combines an artemisinin derivative with a partner drug.
- **Other species (such as *P. vivax*):** Treated with medicines suitable for the species, often including chloroquine in areas where it still works, or ACTs. For *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*, an additional medicine (primaquine or tafenoquine) may be used to clear dormant liver-stage parasites and prevent relapse, after checking for G6PD deficiency.
- **Severe malaria:** Needs hospital care. WHO recommends injectable artesunate, followed by a full course of an oral antimalarial once the patient can take it, along with supportive care (fluids, treating anaemia, low blood sugar, breathing or kidney problems).
- **Pregnancy and young children:** Treatment choices differ and must be set by a doctor.
- **Complete the course:** Take all medicines exactly as prescribed, even if you feel better.
- **Drug resistance:** Parasites can become resistant to antimalarial drugs, including partial resistance to artemisinin reported in parts of Africa and the Greater Mekong subregion. This is why using the correct, complete treatment and using confirmed diagnosis matters.
- **Supportive care at home (for mild, confirmed cases under medical guidance):** Rest, fluids, and fever management as advised by a doctor.
- **Follow-up:** Return to a health provider if symptoms persist or return, if vomiting prevents taking medicine, or if the person worsens.
- **In India,** diagnosis and treatment of malaria are provided free of charge at government health facilities under the national vector-borne disease control programme.

## 9. Prevention
- **Insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs / long-lasting insecticidal nets):** Sleep under a net every night in malaria areas.
- **Indoor residual spraying (IRS):** Spraying insecticide on interior walls, as organised by health authorities.
- **Reduce mosquito bites:** Use mosquito repellents (such as those recommended by health authorities), wear long sleeves and trousers at dusk and dawn, and use window and door screens, fans or air conditioning.
- **Eliminate breeding sites:** Drain or cover stagnant water, clean water storage containers, and support community mosquito control (larval source management).
- **Preventive medicines (chemoprevention):**
  - *Travellers:* Doctors can prescribe antimalarial prophylaxis for people travelling to malaria-risk areas. The choice depends on destination and personal health, so consult a doctor or travel clinic before travelling.
  - *Pregnant women, infants and children in certain high-transmission regions:* WHO recommends targeted chemoprevention programmes (for example, intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy and seasonal malaria chemoprevention for children in certain regions).
- **Vaccines:** WHO recommends the malaria vaccines RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M for children in areas with moderate to high malaria transmission. These vaccines are used alongside, not instead of, other prevention measures. They are not general travel vaccines.
- **Early testing and treatment** of fever in malaria areas reduces spread and complications.
- **Travellers:** Remember that symptoms can appear weeks to months after returning. Tell your doctor about recent travel if you develop fever.
- **No vaccine fully replaces** nets, repellents and other protection.

## 10. Complications
- **Severe malaria (mostly *P. falciparum*):** Can affect several organs.
- **Cerebral malaria:** Brain involvement causing confusion, seizures, coma. It can cause lasting neurological problems.
- **Severe anaemia:** Destruction of red blood cells; especially serious in young children and pregnant women.
- **Respiratory distress / fluid in the lungs**
- **Acute kidney injury**
- **Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)**
- **Jaundice and liver problems**
- **Enlarged spleen, and rarely spleen rupture**
- **Complications in pregnancy:** Miscarriage, stillbirth, premature birth, low birth weight, and maternal anaemia and illness.
- **Relapse:** *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* can leave dormant forms in the liver, which can cause the illness to return weeks or months later without a new mosquito bite.
- **Death** if severe malaria is not treated promptly.
- **Long-term effects:** In some children, learning or developmental difficulties after severe disease.

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Children under 5 years living in high-transmission areas (they account for a large share of malaria deaths, according to WHO)
- Pregnant women
- Infants
- People living with HIV/AIDS
- People with no or low immunity, such as travellers from areas without malaria and migrants returning to their home areas after long absence
- People with a damaged or absent spleen
- People living in remote, rural or poorly served areas, or in humanitarian emergencies, with limited access to nets and treatment
- Forest workers, miners, farm workers and others working outdoors at night in malaria areas
- Mobile and migrant populations

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Any fever if you live in, or have visited, a malaria-risk area. Get tested promptly. Do not wait for it to pass.
- Fever up to a year after returning from a malaria-risk area (tell the doctor about your travel)
- Fever with chills, sweats, headache and body aches that repeat
- Fever in a pregnant woman or a young child in a malaria area
- Persistent vomiting or being unable to take medicine
- Fever returns after treatment
- **Go to emergency care immediately** for confusion, seizures, breathing difficulty, severe weakness, very dark urine, jaundice, or unusual bleeding.
- Before travelling to a malaria-risk area, to discuss preventive medicines and mosquito protection.

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Malaria spreads from person to person like a cold."** It spreads mainly through mosquito bites, not by casual contact.
2. **"All mosquitoes spread malaria."** Only certain female *Anopheles* mosquitoes do.
3. **"Malaria only happens in Africa."** It also occurs in Asia (including India), Latin America, and other regions.
4. **"I can tell it's malaria by the symptoms."** Malaria looks like many other fevers. A blood test is needed.
5. **"Once you've had malaria you're immune for life."** Immunity is partial, slowly acquired and can fade; people can get malaria repeatedly.
6. **"Malaria is mild and always goes away by itself."** It can become severe or fatal quickly, especially *P. falciparum*.
7. **"I can stop medicines when the fever goes away."** Incomplete treatment can lead to relapse or drug resistance.
8. **"A malaria vaccine means I don't need a net or other protection."** Vaccines give partial protection and are used together with other measures.
9. **"Taking antimalarial tablets at the first fever without testing is fine."** Self-treatment can be unsafe, delay the right diagnosis, and add to drug resistance.
10. **"Malaria cannot appear after I return from travel."** Symptoms can start weeks, months or even later after leaving a malaria area.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is malaria?**
Malaria is a disease caused by *Plasmodium* parasites, spread through the bite of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. It causes fever and flu-like illness and can become life-threatening if untreated.

**Q2. How do you get malaria?**
Mainly from the bite of an infected mosquito. Rarely, it can pass from mother to baby, or through blood transfusion, organ transplant or shared needles.

**Q3. Is malaria contagious?**
No, not in the usual sense. It does not spread by touching, coughing, or sharing food. It needs a mosquito (or rare blood-to-blood exposure) to pass on.

**Q4. What are the symptoms of malaria?**
Fever, chills, sweats, headache, body aches, tiredness, nausea and vomiting. Some people have cycles of chills, fever and sweating.

**Q5. How soon after a mosquito bite do symptoms appear?**
Usually about 10 to 15 days, but it can be shorter or much longer depending on the parasite and whether preventive medicine was used.

**Q6. How is malaria diagnosed?**
By a blood test, either microscopy of a blood smear or a rapid diagnostic test, sometimes confirmed by molecular tests.

**Q7. Is malaria curable?**
Yes. With early, correct treatment, most people recover fully.

**Q8. What is the treatment for malaria?**
Treatment uses antimalarial medicines chosen by a doctor according to the parasite species, severity and the patient's situation. Uncomplicated *P. falciparum* malaria is generally treated with artemisinin-based combination therapy, and severe malaria needs hospital care with injectable artesunate. Do not self-medicate.

**Q9. What is severe malaria?**
It is malaria with organ complications such as confusion or seizures (cerebral malaria), severe anaemia, breathing difficulty, kidney failure or low blood sugar. It is an emergency.

**Q10. Why can malaria come back after treatment?**
With *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*, dormant parasites in the liver can reactivate later and cause relapse. Doctors may add a medicine to prevent this. Incomplete treatment and drug resistance can also cause illness to return.

**Q11. How can I prevent malaria?**
Sleep under an insecticide-treated net, use repellents, wear protective clothing at dusk and dawn, remove stagnant water, support spraying programmes, and use preventive medicine where recommended (for example, for some travellers).

**Q12. Is there a vaccine for malaria?**
Yes. WHO recommends the RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M vaccines for children in areas with moderate to high transmission. They provide partial protection and are used with other prevention measures.

**Q13. Should I take antimalarial tablets when travelling?**
It depends on where you are going and your health. Consult a doctor or travel clinic before travel to choose suitable preventive medicine and to learn about protection against bites.

**Q14. Why is malaria dangerous in pregnancy and for children?**
Pregnant women and young children have lower or reduced immunity to the parasite, so illness can become severe, and in pregnancy it can also harm the baby.

**Q15. Where can I get malaria testing and treatment in India?**
At government health facilities, including primary health centres and hospitals, where diagnosis and treatment are available free of charge under the national programme. Ask at your nearest health centre or a health worker (such as an ASHA worker).
