# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Measles
Medical Name: Measles (Rubeola), caused by the measles virus
Disease Category: Infectious disease (viral); airborne; vaccine-preventable; childhood rash illness
Also Known As: Rubeola, "red measles", "hard measles" (older terms), "Khasra" or "Chhoti Mata/Mata" in some parts of India (local names; regional terms can overlap with other rash illnesses such as chickenpox, so use a doctor's diagnosis)

## 1. Overview
Measles is a **highly contagious** viral disease that mainly affects children but can affect anyone who is not immune. It causes high fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes and a characteristic rash.

Measles can lead to **serious complications**, including ear infections, pneumonia, brain inflammation, blindness, and death, especially in young children, malnourished children, and people with weakened immunity. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), measles remains a leading cause of vaccine-preventable death among children worldwide, and outbreaks occur where vaccination coverage is low. See WHO's current pages for the latest figures.

Measles is **preventable with a safe and effective vaccine**. There is no specific antiviral treatment; care focuses on supporting the patient and preventing complications. Because measles spreads so easily, high vaccination coverage in communities is essential to stop outbreaks. India is part of global and regional efforts to eliminate measles and rubella.

## 2. Causes
- Measles is caused by the **measles virus**, a member of the *Morbillivirus* genus (paramyxovirus family). Humans are the only natural host.
- The virus infects the respiratory tract first, then spreads through the body.
- Measles infection weakens the immune system for weeks to months afterward (sometimes called "immune amnesia"), making people more prone to other infections.
- One infection normally gives lifelong immunity.

## 3. Risk Factors
**Risk of infection:**
- Not being vaccinated, or having had only one dose, or an incomplete vaccination schedule
- Never having had measles
- Close contact with someone with measles. Measles is among the most contagious infectious diseases; a person who is not immune and is exposed to an infected person is very likely to become infected.
- Living in or travelling to areas with measles outbreaks or low vaccination coverage
- Crowded settings (schools, hostels, camps, healthcare waiting rooms)
- Children aged under 12 months who are too young to be vaccinated
- Vaccine hesitancy, missed vaccinations, or limited access to health services

**Risk of severe disease and complications:**
- Young age (especially children under 5 years, and infants)
- Malnutrition
- Vitamin A deficiency
- Weakened immune system (for example, HIV, cancer, certain medicines)
- Pregnancy (non-immune)
- Living in overcrowded conditions or in areas with poor healthcare access
- Older adults who are not immune (less common)

## 4. Transmission
- **Airborne spread:** The virus spreads through the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes or breathes. The virus can **remain in the air and on surfaces for up to about 2 hours** after the infected person has left a room, so people can be infected without direct contact.
- **Direct contact:** With nose or throat secretions of an infected person.
- **Very high contagiousness:** Most non-immune people who are exposed become infected.
- **Incubation period:** Usually about **10 to 14 days** from exposure to the start of symptoms (range about 7 to 21 days), and about 14 days to the appearance of the rash.
- **Infectious period:** A person is contagious from about **4 days before the rash appears until about 4 days after**. People with weakened immunity may be contagious for longer.
- **Not spread by:** Food or water.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
Measles typically develops in stages:
- **Early (prodromal) phase, about 2 to 4 days:**
  - High fever (often rising to 40 °C / 104 °F or more)
  - Cough
  - Runny nose
  - Red, watery eyes (conjunctivitis) and sensitivity to light
  - Tiny white spots with bluish-white centres on the inside of the cheeks (**Koplik spots**), which may appear 1 to 2 days before the rash
  - Tiredness and irritability
- **Rash phase:**
  - A flat red or reddish-brown blotchy rash that usually begins on the **face and along the hairline**, then spreads down to the neck, trunk, arms, legs and feet
  - Spots may join together; the rash typically lasts about 5 to 6 days and then fades
  - Fever often peaks around the time the rash appears

### Less Common Symptoms
- Loss of appetite
- Diarrhoea and vomiting (especially in young children)
- Muscle aches
- Mouth sores
- Swollen glands
- Ear pain
- Mild or atypical measles in people who are partially immune (for example, those who have received only some vaccine protection)

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Fever with cough, runny nose and red eyes, particularly in an unvaccinated child or after contact with someone with measles
- A rash with fever
- Fever lasting more than 3 to 4 days, or returning after improving
- Ear pain or discharge
- Fast breathing, wheeze, or persistent cough
- Diarrhoea, vomiting, or signs of dehydration
- Eye pain or sensitivity to light
- Measles in infants, pregnant women, people with weakened immunity, or malnourished children

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital or call local emergency services) if someone with measles or suspected measles has:
- Difficulty breathing, fast breathing, chest indrawing, or bluish lips
- Persistent high fever that does not settle
- Severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, drowsiness, difficulty waking, convulsions (seizures)
- Weakness or loss of consciousness
- Persistent vomiting or severe diarrhoea, signs of dehydration (very dry mouth, no tears, very little urine, sunken eyes)
- Eye pain, clouding of the eye, or loss of vision
- Refusal to drink or breastfeed
- Ear discharge with high fever
- Signs of severe illness in an infant, malnourished child, pregnant woman, or immunocompromised person

## 7. Diagnosis
- **Clinical suspicion:** A doctor or health worker suspects measles from the typical combination of fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes and a rash that starts on the face, especially if the person is unvaccinated or has been exposed.
- **Laboratory confirmation:** Measles is confirmed with laboratory tests, particularly during outbreaks and for surveillance:
  - **Blood tests for measles-specific IgM antibodies** (show recent infection)
  - **PCR (RT-PCR) testing** of throat or nose swabs, or urine, to detect the virus's genetic material
  - Virus culture or genotyping in specialised laboratories
- **Reporting:** Measles is a **notifiable disease**. Suspected cases are reported to public health authorities, which investigate to find contacts and prevent further spread.
- **Other tests:** Chest X-ray, blood tests and other investigations may be done if complications are suspected.
- Measles can look like other rash illnesses (such as rubella, dengue, or drug reactions), so testing helps identify the cause.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Treatment decisions are made by a doctor or health worker based on the person's age, condition and any complications. Do not self-medicate.*

- **No specific antiviral treatment exists.** Care is supportive, aimed at easing symptoms and preventing or treating complications.
- **Rest and fluids:** Plenty of rest and fluids. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) may be advised if there is diarrhoea or dehydration.
- **Fever and discomfort:** Medicines for fever can be used as advised by a healthcare professional. **Do not give aspirin to children and teenagers** because of the risk of Reye's syndrome.
- **Nutrition and breastfeeding:** Continue breastfeeding and offer age-appropriate, nutritious food as tolerated.
- **Eye care:** Gently clean eyes with clean water if there is discharge; ask a doctor about any eye symptoms.
- **Vitamin A supplementation:** WHO recommends that **all children with measles receive vitamin A** under health worker guidance. This can help reduce the risk of eye damage, blindness and death. The dose and schedule depend on age and are given by a health worker. Do not give vitamin A supplements on your own.
- **Antibiotics:** Do not treat measles itself, since it is a virus. A doctor may prescribe antibiotics for complications such as bacterial ear infections or pneumonia.
- **Hospital care:** Needed for severe illness or complications such as pneumonia, dehydration, brain involvement, or in high-risk patients. Hospital treatment may include oxygen, fluids and monitoring.
- **Isolation:** Stay home and away from others during the contagious period, especially away from infants, pregnant women and people with weakened immunity, in line with local public health guidance. Return to school or work only when a doctor says it is safe, usually at least 4 days after the rash appears.
- **After exposure:** Non-immune people who have been exposed, especially high-risk people (infants, pregnant women, people with weakened immunity), should contact a doctor immediately. In some cases, measles vaccine (within about 72 hours of exposure) or immune globulin (within about 6 days) can help prevent or reduce illness. A doctor decides.
- **Recovery:** Most people recover within about 2 to 3 weeks, but recovery can be slower in malnourished or immunocompromised people.
- **In India,** measles testing and care are available at government health facilities. Report suspected cases to the nearest health centre so public health teams can respond.

## 9. Prevention
- **Vaccination is the most effective way to prevent measles.** The vaccine is safe and effective, and is given as **two doses** in routine immunisation programmes. It is often given as a combined vaccine: measles-rubella (MR) or measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), depending on the country.
- **Typical schedule in India:** Under the national immunisation programme, **MR vaccine is given at 9 to 12 months and a second dose at 16 to 24 months**. Check with a health worker or doctor for the current schedule and for catch-up vaccination for older children and adults.
- **Catch-up and outbreak vaccination:** Children and adults who missed vaccination or have not had measles should ask their doctor about vaccination. Campaigns (mass vaccination drives) are carried out in areas at risk.
- **High community coverage (herd immunity):** Because measles is so contagious, about 95% or more of a community needs to be fully immunised to prevent outbreaks. This protects babies too young to be vaccinated and people who cannot be vaccinated.
- **Who should not receive the vaccine:** The measles vaccine is a live vaccine, so it is generally not given during pregnancy or to people with severely weakened immunity. People who cannot be vaccinated rely on others being vaccinated. Ask a doctor about whether the vaccine is suitable.
- **Vitamin A:** Vitamin A supplements, as part of national programmes, support child health but do not replace vaccination.
- **Isolation of cases:** People with measles should stay home until the contagious period is over.
- **Contact tracing:** Public health teams identify and vaccinate contacts to stop spread.
- **Travellers:** Make sure you and your children are up to date with measles vaccination before travel, especially to areas with outbreaks.
- **Hygiene and ventilation:** Cover coughs and sneezes and keep rooms well ventilated; these measures help but do not replace vaccination.

## 10. Complications
Complications are more common in children under 5, adults over 20, malnourished children and people with weakened immunity.
- **Ear infection (otitis media):** A common complication that can lead to hearing loss
- **Diarrhoea and dehydration**
- **Pneumonia:** The most common cause of measles-related death in young children
- **Croup, bronchitis**
- **Eye problems:** Corneal damage, and blindness, especially in children with vitamin A deficiency
- **Encephalitis (brain inflammation):** Can cause seizures, deafness, intellectual disability or death
- **Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE):** A rare, fatal brain disease that can appear years after measles infection
- **Immune suppression ("immune amnesia"):** Increased risk of other infections for weeks to months, or possibly longer
- **Malnutrition:** Weight loss and worsening of undernutrition
- **Pregnancy complications:** Miscarriage, premature birth or low birth weight, and severe illness in the mother
- **Death:** Measles can be fatal, mainly through pneumonia, brain involvement and dehydration, particularly in young children and in settings with poor healthcare access

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- Unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated children
- Infants under 12 months, who are too young to be fully protected by vaccination
- Malnourished children and children with vitamin A deficiency
- People with weakened immunity (HIV, cancer, chemotherapy, transplant, long-term steroids)
- Pregnant women who are not immune
- Adults born without immunity who are unvaccinated
- People living in crowded or poorly resourced settings, camps or slums
- People in or travelling to areas with outbreaks or low vaccination coverage
- Health workers who are not immune
- Children and communities with limited access to vaccination or health services

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Fever with cough, runny nose, red eyes and a rash, **especially if the person is unvaccinated or has been in contact with someone with measles**. Call ahead to the clinic so staff can take precautions to avoid spreading measles in the waiting area.
- A suspected measles case in an infant, pregnant woman, malnourished child, or person with weakened immunity
- Contact with someone with measles, if you or your child are not immune
- Fever lasting more than 3 to 4 days, or returning after improving
- Ear pain, cough with fast breathing, persistent diarrhoea or vomiting
- Any warning sign in Section 6 (**go to hospital immediately**)
- Eye pain, redness or vision changes
- To check your or your child's vaccination status and complete missed doses
- Before travel to areas with measles outbreaks

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Measles is just a harmless childhood illness."** Measles can cause serious complications and death, especially in young children and malnourished people.
2. **"The measles vaccine causes autism."** Large studies and health authorities including WHO and CDC have found no link between measles-containing vaccines and autism.
3. **"Natural infection is better than vaccination."** Natural measles infection can cause severe complications and weaken the immune system. Vaccination provides safe protection without these risks.
4. **"Measles has disappeared, so vaccination isn't needed."** Measles still occurs and outbreaks happen where vaccination coverage drops. Vaccination remains necessary.
5. **"Only children get measles."** Anyone who is not immune can get measles, including teenagers and adults, and illness can be severe in adults.
6. **"Vitamin A prevents measles."** Vitamin A does not prevent measles. It is given to children who have measles, as advised by health workers, to reduce the risk of severe complications. Vaccination prevents measles.
7. **"Measles is caused by a goddess's anger or a curse."** Measles is an infection caused by a virus. Early medical care and vaccination matter. Cultural practices should not replace medical care.
8. **"A vaccinated person can never get measles."** Two doses are highly effective but not 100% protective; breakthrough cases are rare and usually milder.
9. **"Antibiotics cure measles."** Antibiotics do not treat viruses. They are used only for bacterial complications as decided by a doctor.
10. **"Once the rash appears, the person isn't contagious."** A person can spread measles for several days after the rash appears (about 4 days).

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is measles?**
Measles is a highly contagious viral illness that causes fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes and a characteristic rash. It can lead to serious complications.

**Q2. How does measles spread?**
Through the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes or breathes. The virus can stay in the air or on surfaces for up to about 2 hours, so people can get infected without direct contact.

**Q3. How contagious is measles?**
Extremely contagious. Most people who are not immune and are exposed to measles will become infected.

**Q4. What are the symptoms of measles?**
High fever, cough, runny nose and red watery eyes, followed by a red blotchy rash that starts on the face and spreads downwards. Small white spots inside the cheeks (Koplik spots) may appear before the rash.

**Q5. How long after exposure do symptoms appear?**
Usually about 10 to 14 days, within a range of about 7 to 21 days. The rash usually appears around 14 days after exposure.

**Q6. When is a person with measles contagious?**
From about 4 days before the rash appears to about 4 days after it appears.

**Q7. How is measles diagnosed?**
By a doctor's assessment and laboratory tests, mainly blood tests for measles IgM antibodies and PCR tests on throat, nose or urine samples. Measles is a notifiable disease, so cases are reported to health authorities.

**Q8. Is there a treatment for measles?**
There is no specific antiviral treatment. Care involves rest, fluids, treating fever and complications, and, for children, vitamin A as directed by a health worker. Do not self-medicate.

**Q9. Why is vitamin A given to children with measles?**
WHO recommends vitamin A for all children with measles because it can reduce the risk of eye damage, blindness and death. It must be given under health worker guidance.

**Q10. What complications can measles cause?**
Ear infections, diarrhoea, pneumonia, brain inflammation, blindness, and death. It also weakens the immune system for a period after infection. A rare brain disease (SSPE) can appear years later.

**Q11. How can I prevent measles?**
Get vaccinated. Two doses of a measles-containing vaccine (such as MR or MMR) give strong protection. Keep infected people at home until they are no longer contagious.

**Q12. When should my child get the measles vaccine in India?**
Under India's national immunisation programme, MR vaccine is given at 9 to 12 months, with a second dose at 16 to 24 months. Ask a health worker or doctor to confirm the current schedule and arrange catch-up doses if any were missed.

**Q13. Is the measles vaccine safe?**
Yes. It has been used for decades with an excellent safety record. Mild side effects such as fever, mild rash or soreness can occur. Serious reactions are very rare. It does not cause autism.

**Q14. Who should not get the measles vaccine?**
The vaccine is a live vaccine, so it is generally not given during pregnancy or to people with severely weakened immunity. A doctor can advise whether it is suitable.

**Q15. What should I do if my child has been exposed to measles?**
Contact a doctor promptly, especially if the child is an infant, unvaccinated, or has weakened immunity. In some cases, a vaccine given within about 3 days, or immune globulin given within about 6 days, can help. Call ahead before visiting a clinic so that staff can prevent spread.
