# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Tuberculosis (TB)
Medical Name: Tuberculosis (caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (bacterial); airborne; primarily affects the lungs
Also Known As: TB, Koch's disease, consumption (historical term), phthisis (historical term), "white plague" (historical term)

## 1. Overview
Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by bacteria called *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. It most often affects the lungs (pulmonary TB) but can affect other parts of the body such as the lymph nodes, bones, spine, kidneys, brain and its coverings (extrapulmonary TB).

TB spreads through the air when a person with active TB disease in the lungs or throat coughs, sneezes, speaks or sings. TB is preventable and, in most cases, curable with the right antibiotics taken for the full course.

There are two main states:
- **Latent TB infection (LTBI):** The person has TB bacteria in the body but the immune system keeps them under control. The person has no symptoms and cannot spread TB. LTBI can become active TB disease later, so preventive treatment is often offered to people at higher risk.
- **Active TB disease:** The bacteria are multiplying and causing illness. The person usually has symptoms and, if the disease is in the lungs or throat, can spread TB to others.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), TB is one of the world's leading causes of death from a single infectious agent, and a large share of the global burden falls on low- and middle-income countries, including India. See the latest WHO Global TB Report for current figures.

## 2. Causes
- TB is caused by bacteria of the *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex, most commonly *M. tuberculosis*.
- The bacteria are spread person to person through tiny airborne droplets (droplet nuclei) released by people with infectious TB disease.
- Having the bacteria in the body does not always mean illness. Whether TB disease develops depends largely on the strength of the person's immune system.

## 3. Risk Factors
Risk factors fall into two groups: those that increase the chance of **getting infected**, and those that increase the chance of **developing disease once infected**.

**Increasing chance of infection (exposure):**
- Close or prolonged contact with someone who has infectious TB (household members, close contacts)
- Living or working in crowded or poorly ventilated places (some prisons, shelters, congregate settings)
- Working in healthcare or other settings with exposure to TB patients
- Living in or travelling to regions where TB is common

**Increasing chance of progressing to TB disease:**
- HIV infection
- Diabetes
- Undernutrition / low body weight
- Tobacco smoking
- Harmful alcohol use
- Treatments or conditions that weaken immunity (for example, long-term corticosteroids, TNF-alpha blocker drugs, organ transplant medicines, some cancers, chemotherapy)
- Chronic kidney disease
- Silicosis and some other chronic lung conditions
- Very young age (infants and young children)
- Recent infection (within the past two years)

## 4. Transmission
- **How it spreads:** Through the air. A person with infectious pulmonary or laryngeal TB releases bacteria when coughing, sneezing, speaking or singing. Others can breathe these in.
- **Most likely scenarios:** Prolonged, repeated or close contact in enclosed, poorly ventilated spaces (such as living in the same household).
- **How it does NOT spread:** Shaking hands, sharing food or drinks, touching surfaces, sharing bedding or toilets, kissing casually, or sharing clothes. Latent TB infection is not contagious.
- **Infectiousness:** People become much less infectious after starting effective treatment, typically within a few weeks, but they must follow their healthcare provider's advice on when it is safe to resume normal contact.
- **Non-lung TB:** TB disease outside the lungs (for example in lymph nodes, bones or the spine) is generally not infectious, unless the lungs are also involved.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
For TB of the lungs:
- Cough lasting more than 2 to 3 weeks (WHO and national programmes use this as a prompt for testing)
- Coughing up sputum (phlegm), sometimes with blood
- Chest pain
- Fever
- Night sweats
- Unintentional weight loss
- Loss of appetite
- Tiredness / weakness
- Chills

### Less Common Symptoms
Symptoms depend on which part of the body is affected (extrapulmonary TB):
- **Lymph node TB:** Painless swelling of glands, often in the neck
- **Spinal / bone TB:** Back pain, stiffness, bone or joint pain, sometimes deformity
- **Kidney or urinary TB:** Blood in the urine, flank pain
- **TB of the abdomen:** Abdominal pain, swelling, changes in bowel habits
- **TB meningitis:** Persistent headache, fever, vomiting, neck stiffness, confusion
- **Children:** Poor weight gain, persistent fever, fatigue, reduced playfulness, and a cough that does not go away

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- A cough lasting more than 2 to 3 weeks
- Coughing up blood or blood-streaked sputum
- Unexplained weight loss or night sweats
- Fever that keeps returning for weeks
- Having been in close contact with someone diagnosed with TB and developing any of the above
- Persistent swelling of glands, or back or bone pain without a clear cause

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital or call local emergency services) if any of these occur:
- Coughing up a large amount of blood
- Severe difficulty breathing, bluish lips or face, or severe chest pain
- Severe headache with neck stiffness, confusion, drowsiness, seizures or fainting (possible TB meningitis)
- Sudden weakness or loss of movement in the legs, or loss of bladder/bowel control (possible spinal TB complication)
- **While on TB treatment:** yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), dark urine, persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, severe rash, or vision changes. These can be signs of serious side effects, and the treating doctor should be contacted promptly. Do not stop medicines on your own.

## 7. Diagnosis
TB can only be confirmed through medical tests ordered by a healthcare professional. Common approaches include:
- **Medical history and physical examination:** Symptoms, exposure history, and risk factors.
- **Sputum tests (for pulmonary TB):**
  - *Smear microscopy:* Looks for TB bacteria under the microscope.
  - *Molecular tests (for example, Xpert MTB/RIF or similar WHO-recommended rapid tests):* Detect TB bacteria and often also detect resistance to rifampicin within hours. WHO recommends rapid molecular tests as the initial test where available.
  - *Culture:* Grows the bacteria in a laboratory; slower but sensitive, and used for drug susceptibility testing.
- **Chest X-ray:** Helps identify lung changes suggestive of TB; supports but does not on its own confirm the diagnosis.
- **Drug susceptibility testing:** Determines whether the bacteria are resistant to certain TB medicines.
- **Tests for TB infection (for latent TB):**
  - *Tuberculin skin test (TST / Mantoux test)*
  - *Interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) blood test*
  
  These tests show whether a person has been infected with TB bacteria. They **cannot** tell whether the infection is latent or active, and a positive result is followed by further evaluation, such as a symptom review and chest X-ray.
- **Other samples:** For extrapulmonary TB, samples such as tissue (biopsy), lymph node aspirate, spinal fluid, urine, or other fluids may be tested, sometimes with imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI).
- **HIV testing** is recommended for all people diagnosed with TB, and diabetes screening is also advised, because these conditions affect care.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. Specific drugs, doses and durations are decided only by a qualified doctor or national TB programme.*

- **TB is curable.** Treatment uses a combination of antibiotics taken for several months. Using more than one drug at a time helps prevent drug resistance.
- **Drug-susceptible TB:** The standard regimen has typically been about 6 months, using a combination of first-line medicines (such as isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol). WHO also recommends a shorter 4-month regimen for eligible patients in some circumstances.
- **Drug-resistant TB (DR-TB, including MDR/RR-TB):** Requires different, specially designed regimens. WHO now recommends shorter, all-oral regimens for many patients, and treatment is supervised by specialists.
- **Latent TB infection:** Preventive treatment (TB preventive therapy) with shorter regimens may be offered to people at higher risk of developing active TB, such as household contacts of TB patients and people with HIV.
- **Adherence is critical:** Taking every dose for the full course, even after feeling better, prevents relapse and drug resistance. Many programmes, including India's National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP), provide free diagnosis and treatment and support adherence (for example, through treatment supporters and digital adherence tools).
- **Side effects:** Possible side effects include nausea, loss of appetite, joint pain, skin rash, tingling in the hands or feet, and liver problems. Report any symptoms to the healthcare provider rather than stopping the medicine.
- **Nutrition and support:** Good nutrition supports recovery. Many countries offer nutritional or financial support schemes for TB patients (for example, India's Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana).
- **Infection control while infectious:** Cover the mouth when coughing, keep rooms well ventilated, spend time outdoors where possible, and follow the doctor's instructions on isolation and when it is safe to return to work or school.
- **Follow-up:** Regular monitoring by a health provider (sputum checks, symptom review) to confirm treatment is working.

## 9. Prevention
- **Early diagnosis and complete treatment** of people with TB is the most important way to stop spread.
- **BCG vaccine:** Given to infants in many countries with high TB burden (including India). It helps protect young children against severe forms of TB (such as TB meningitis) but offers variable protection against adult pulmonary TB.
- **Contact tracing and screening:** Household and close contacts of TB patients should be screened by health services.
- **TB preventive treatment:** For eligible high-risk contacts and people with HIV, as determined by a doctor.
- **Good ventilation:** Open windows, use airflow, and avoid crowding in enclosed spaces.
- **Cough etiquette:** Cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing; wearing a well-fitting mask while infectious and in crowded or healthcare settings.
- **Manage risk factors:** Treat HIV and diabetes, avoid tobacco, limit alcohol, and maintain good nutrition.
- **Infection control in healthcare settings:** Triage, isolation of infectious patients, and appropriate respirators for health workers.
- **Reduce stigma:** Encouraging people to seek testing early helps limit spread.

## 10. Complications
- **If untreated:** TB disease can be fatal; a substantial proportion of untreated people with active TB die.
- **Lung damage:** Scarring, cavities, bronchiectasis, chronic breathing problems, and in some cases chronic lung disease.
- **Spread to other organs:** Meningitis, bone and spine TB, kidney TB, miliary (widespread) TB.
- **Drug-resistant TB:** Can result from incomplete or inappropriate treatment, and is harder, longer and more costly to treat.
- **Relapse:** If treatment is incomplete.
- **Treatment-related side effects:** Including liver inflammation (hepatitis), nerve problems, skin reactions.
- **Complications with HIV:** TB is a leading cause of death among people living with HIV.
- **Social and economic impact:** Loss of income, stigma, and effects on family.

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- People living with HIV
- Close contacts of people with infectious TB (especially household contacts)
- Children under 5 years (high risk of progressing to severe disease)
- People with diabetes
- People who are undernourished
- People who smoke or use alcohol or drugs harmfully
- People with weakened immunity due to illness or medication
- People with chronic kidney disease, silicosis or other lung disease
- Healthcare workers and others exposed to TB patients
- People in crowded or institutional settings (prisons, shelters, some congregate living)
- People in or from high-TB-burden regions, migrants, and people with limited access to health care
- Older adults (due to reactivation of past infection)

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- A cough that lasts more than 2 to 3 weeks
- Coughing up blood
- Persistent fever, night sweats, unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite
- Swollen glands, or persistent back or bone pain without a clear cause
- Contact with someone diagnosed with TB
- If you are in a higher-risk group (for example, living with HIV or diabetes) and have any symptoms
- Before or during any treatment: if you experience side effects, miss doses, or have questions about your medicines

Where to go: a government health centre, a district TB programme (in India, services are free under NTEP), or a doctor or hospital. A healthcare professional should assess symptoms and order appropriate tests.

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"TB only affects the lungs."** TB mostly affects the lungs, but it can affect almost any organ.
2. **"TB is hereditary."** TB is not inherited. It is an infection caused by bacteria.
3. **"TB spreads by touching, sharing food or utensils."** It spreads through the air, not through casual contact or objects.
4. **"Anyone who has TB infection is contagious."** People with latent TB infection are not contagious.
5. **"TB is incurable."** TB is curable with the right treatment taken fully.
6. **"I can stop medicine once I feel better."** Stopping early can cause relapse and drug-resistant TB.
7. **"TB is only a disease of the poor."** Anyone can get TB, though poverty, undernutrition and crowding raise the risk.
8. **"TB is a thing of the past."** TB remains a major global public health problem.
9. **"The BCG vaccine fully protects against TB."** BCG mainly protects young children from severe forms; protection against adult lung TB is limited.
10. **"People with TB should be isolated forever or shunned."** Effective treatment quickly reduces infectiousness, and stigma delays care.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is tuberculosis?**
TB is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. It usually affects the lungs but can affect other parts of the body.

**Q2. How does TB spread?**
Through the air, when a person with infectious TB of the lungs or throat coughs, sneezes, speaks or sings and others breathe in the bacteria.

**Q3. What is the difference between latent TB and active TB disease?**
In latent TB infection, bacteria are in the body but inactive; the person feels well and is not contagious. In active TB disease, the bacteria are multiplying and usually cause symptoms, and the person may be contagious if the lungs or throat are affected.

**Q4. What are the main symptoms of TB?**
A cough lasting more than 2 to 3 weeks, sometimes with blood, along with chest pain, fever, night sweats, weight loss, loss of appetite and tiredness. Symptoms vary if TB affects other organs.

**Q5. How is TB diagnosed?**
Through medical evaluation and tests such as sputum tests (including rapid molecular tests), chest X-ray, and culture. Skin or blood tests can detect TB infection but cannot tell latent from active disease on their own.

**Q6. Is TB curable?**
Yes. TB can be cured with a full course of the right antibiotics prescribed by a healthcare provider.

**Q7. How long does TB treatment take?**
Standard treatment for drug-susceptible TB usually takes about 6 months, although some shorter regimens exist. Drug-resistant TB regimens differ. The treating doctor decides the exact plan.

**Q8. Why must I complete the full course of TB medicines?**
Stopping early can allow surviving bacteria to multiply and become resistant to drugs, making TB harder to treat and increasing the chance of relapse.

**Q9. When does a person with TB stop being contagious?**
Infectiousness typically falls significantly after effective treatment begins, but the timing varies by person. The healthcare team advises when it is safe to resume normal activities.

**Q10. Can TB be prevented?**
Yes, through early detection and treatment, the BCG vaccine for infants in high-burden countries, preventive treatment for high-risk contacts, good ventilation, cough etiquette and managing risk factors such as HIV and diabetes.

**Q11. Does the BCG vaccine prevent all TB?**
No. BCG mainly protects young children from severe forms of TB, and its protection against adult lung TB is limited.

**Q12. Can I get TB from touching or sharing items with someone who has it?**
TB is not spread by touching surfaces, sharing food, utensils, clothes or bedding. It is spread through the air.

**Q13. Can children and pregnant women get TB?**
Yes. Children, especially under 5, are at higher risk of severe TB. TB in pregnancy needs prompt medical care; anyone who is pregnant and suspects TB should see a doctor right away. TB treatment during pregnancy and breastfeeding should be discussed with the healthcare provider.

**Q14. What is drug-resistant TB?**
It is TB caused by bacteria that no longer respond to one or more standard TB medicines. It can develop when treatment is incomplete or inappropriate, and it can also spread directly from person to person. It needs specialised treatment.

**Q15. Where can I get TB testing and treatment?**
At government health facilities and recognised hospitals. In India, TB diagnosis and treatment are available free of charge through the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP). Ask at the nearest health centre.
