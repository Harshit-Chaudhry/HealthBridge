# DISEASE PROFILE
Disease Name: Typhoid
Medical Name: Typhoid fever (enteric fever caused by *Salmonella enterica* serotype Typhi)
Disease Category: Infectious disease (bacterial); food-borne and water-borne; gastrointestinal / systemic
Also Known As: Typhoid fever, enteric fever, *Salmonella* Typhi infection (a similar illness caused by *Salmonella* Paratyphi is called paratyphoid fever)

## 1. Overview
Typhoid fever is a serious bacterial infection caused by *Salmonella enterica* serotype Typhi (often written *Salmonella* Typhi). It spreads through contaminated food and water and affects the whole body, including the intestines and bloodstream.

Typhoid is common in places where safe drinking water, sanitation and hygiene are limited, including parts of South Asia (including India), Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America. A closely related illness, paratyphoid fever, is caused by *Salmonella* Paratyphi and tends to be similar but often milder. Together they are called enteric fever.

With early diagnosis and the right antibiotics, most people recover. Without treatment, typhoid can cause serious complications and can be fatal. Typhoid is preventable through safe water, sanitation, hygiene, food safety and vaccination. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), typhoid remains an important public health problem, particularly where antibiotic resistance is rising. See WHO's current pages for the latest figures.

## 2. Causes
- Typhoid is caused by the bacterium ***Salmonella* Typhi**, which lives only in humans.
- A person becomes infected by swallowing food or water contaminated with the faeces (and sometimes urine) of an infected person.
- Some people who recover continue to carry the bacteria and can pass it on without symptoms. These people are called **chronic carriers**, and the bacteria often live in the gallbladder.
- Typhoid is different from **typhus**, which is caused by different bacteria and is spread by lice, fleas or mites.

## 3. Risk Factors
- Lack of safe drinking water and poor sanitation
- Drinking untreated or contaminated water, or using ice made from it
- Eating food prepared or handled by someone who has typhoid or is a carrier, or who has poor hand hygiene
- Eating raw or poorly cooked street food, unwashed raw fruits and vegetables, or unpasteurised dairy
- Poor handwashing, especially after using the toilet and before handling food
- Close contact with someone who has typhoid or is a carrier
- Living in or travelling to areas where typhoid is common
- Flooding, overcrowding and breakdown of water or sewage systems
- Poor access to vaccination and healthcare
- Young age (school-age children and young children are often most affected in endemic areas)

## 4. Transmission
- **Faecal-oral route:** The bacteria pass out in the stools (and sometimes urine) of infected people. If these contaminate water or food, others who swallow them can become infected.
- **Contaminated water:** Sewage-contaminated drinking water is a major source.
- **Contaminated food:** Food handled by infected people or carriers, or washed or prepared with contaminated water.
- **Direct contact:** Less commonly, through contact with an infected person's contaminated hands or objects.
- **Carriers:** People without symptoms can spread the bacteria for a long time, which is why safe food-handling matters.
- **Incubation period:** Symptoms usually start about 6 to 30 days after exposure (often 1 to 3 weeks).
- **Contagiousness:** A person can pass on typhoid bacteria while unwell and, if untreated or if they become a carrier, for longer.

## 5. Symptoms

### Common Symptoms
Typhoid often builds up slowly. Symptoms may include:
- A fever that rises gradually over several days and can reach about 39 to 40 °C (102 to 104 °F)
- Weakness and tiredness
- Headache
- Stomach pain
- Loss of appetite
- Constipation or diarrhoea (constipation is more often seen in adults, diarrhoea in children)

### Less Common Symptoms
- Cough
- Muscle aches
- Nausea and vomiting
- Sweating
- A faint rash of flat, rose-coloured spots on the chest or abdomen ("rose spots")
- Slow heart rate relative to the fever
- Enlarged spleen or liver
- Confusion, delirium or a dull, "typhoid state" in more severe cases
- Swollen belly

### Symptoms That May Require Medical Attention
- Fever lasting more than a few days, especially with stomach pain, weakness or headache
- Fever after drinking untreated water or after travel to a typhoid-affected area
- Persistent diarrhoea, constipation or vomiting
- Signs of dehydration (dry mouth, very little urine, dizziness)
- Fever in a child, pregnant woman, or older adult
- Fever returning after seeming to get better

## 6. Warning Signs / Emergency Symptoms
Seek urgent medical care immediately (go to the nearest hospital) if someone with fever or suspected typhoid has:
- Severe or worsening abdominal pain, a rigid or swollen belly (possible intestinal perforation)
- Blood in the stool, black stools, or vomiting blood (possible intestinal bleeding)
- Confusion, severe drowsiness, delirium, difficulty waking, or seizures
- Persistent vomiting or being unable to drink fluids
- Signs of severe dehydration or shock (cold clammy skin, fast weak pulse, fainting, very little urine)
- Very high fever that does not come down, or severe weakness
- Difficulty breathing
- Yellowing of the skin or eyes
- A newborn, infant or young child with fever and poor feeding or unusual sleepiness

Serious complications such as bleeding or perforation of the intestine most often appear in the **second to third week** of untreated illness, so worsening illness needs urgent attention.

## 7. Diagnosis
Typhoid cannot be reliably diagnosed from symptoms alone because many fevers look similar. A healthcare professional will use laboratory tests.
- **Blood culture:** Growing the bacteria from a blood sample is the commonly used diagnostic test, most useful in the early stages and before antibiotics are started. It also allows antibiotic sensitivity testing.
- **Bone marrow culture:** More sensitive but rarely needed; used in some difficult cases.
- **Stool and urine culture:** Can detect the bacteria, mostly useful later in the illness and for identifying carriers.
- **Widal test:** An older antibody test still used in some places. It has important limitations (it can be falsely positive or negative, and results can be hard to interpret in areas where typhoid is common), so it should be interpreted by a doctor and not relied on alone.
- **Rapid antibody tests and molecular tests:** Available in some settings; their reliability varies, and positive or negative results are interpreted by a doctor with the rest of the clinical picture.
- **Antibiotic sensitivity testing:** Important because drug-resistant strains are common in some regions.
- **Other tests:** Blood counts, liver tests and others to look for complications or other causes of fever.

## 8. Treatment and Management
*This section is general education. The right antibiotic, dose and duration are decided only by a doctor, based on the person's condition, local resistance patterns, and test results. Do not self-medicate.*

- **Typhoid is treatable with antibiotics.** Prompt treatment lowers the chance of complications and death.
- **Antibiotic choice:** Depends on the person's age, severity, pregnancy, local resistance patterns and laboratory sensitivity results.
- **Antibiotic resistance is a serious concern:** Strains resistant to several commonly used antibiotics (multidrug-resistant) and, in some regions, **extensively drug-resistant (XDR)** strains have been reported. Fluoroquinolone resistance is common in parts of South Asia. This makes it especially important not to take antibiotics without medical advice and to complete prescribed courses.
- **Complete the full course:** Stopping early can lead to relapse and can contribute to resistance.
- **Supportive care:** Rest, plenty of fluids (including oral rehydration solution), and easy-to-digest food. Fever control should be done as advised by a doctor.
- **Hospital care:** Needed for severe illness, persistent vomiting, dehydration, confusion, or complications. Serious complications such as intestinal perforation or bleeding may need intensive care or surgery.
- **Recovery time:** Fever often takes several days to settle after proper treatment begins. Full recovery and return of strength can take weeks. Relapse can occur in some people, so follow-up matters.
- **Carriers:** Some people continue to carry the bacteria after recovery. Doctors may advise follow-up stool or other tests, treatment to clear the carrier state, and restricting food handling until cleared.
- **Prevent spreading to others:** Wash hands thoroughly with soap and clean water after using the toilet and before preparing or eating food, and avoid preparing food for others while unwell or until a doctor says it is safe.
- **In India,** typhoid testing and treatment are available at government and private health facilities. Ask at the nearest health centre or hospital.

## 9. Prevention
- **Safe drinking water:** Drink boiled, filtered, bottled or properly treated water. Be cautious about ice and untreated water.
- **Safe food practices:** Eat food that is freshly cooked and served hot. Wash fruits and vegetables with safe water, and peel fruit when appropriate. Avoid raw or undercooked food and unpasteurised dairy where the safety is uncertain.
- **Hand hygiene:** Wash hands with soap and safe water after using the toilet, after changing a child's nappy, and before preparing or eating food.
- **Sanitation:** Safe disposal of human waste, and protecting water sources from contamination.
- **Food handlers:** People with typhoid, or known carriers, should not handle food until cleared by a doctor.
- **Vaccination:** Typhoid vaccines are available. WHO recommends **typhoid conjugate vaccines (TCV)** for use in countries with high typhoid burden, starting from about 6 months of age. Other vaccines include the injectable Vi polysaccharide vaccine and an oral vaccine (Ty21a), whose use and age limits differ by product and country. Vaccination is also advised for many travellers to high-risk areas. Ask a doctor or local health authority which vaccine is suitable and available. **Vaccines are not 100% protective, so safe water and food habits still matter.**
- **Travellers:** Follow the advice "boil it, cook it, peel it, or leave it" for food and drink, and consider vaccination before travel.
- **Early treatment** of cases and follow-up of carriers helps limit community spread.

## 10. Complications
Complications are more likely if treatment is delayed.
- **Intestinal bleeding** (gastrointestinal haemorrhage)
- **Intestinal perforation:** A hole in the intestine that leaks bowel contents into the abdomen, causing peritonitis, a life-threatening emergency
- **Dehydration**
- **Typhoid encephalopathy:** Confusion, delirium or reduced consciousness
- **Spread of the infection to other organs:** Liver, gallbladder, heart (myocarditis), bones and joints, lungs, kidneys, brain or its coverings (meningitis), and others
- **Shock and multi-organ failure** in severe cases
- **Relapse:** Illness can return after initial recovery
- **Chronic carrier state:** Continued shedding of the bacteria
- **Antibiotic-resistant infection**, which can be harder to treat and lengthen illness
- **Death** can occur without treatment, and rarely even with treatment

## 11. High-Risk Groups
- People living in areas with poor water supply and sanitation
- Young children and school-age children in high-burden areas
- Travellers to areas where typhoid is common, especially if unvaccinated
- Close contacts of a person with typhoid or a carrier
- Food handlers and household members of carriers
- Pregnant women (illness can be serious and treatment choices need medical guidance)
- People with weakened immunity, such as those with HIV
- People with gallbladder disease or gallstones (higher risk of becoming carriers)
- People in crowded settings or disaster and flood-affected areas
- People with limited access to healthcare or vaccines
- Older adults and those with other serious illnesses

## 12. When to See a Doctor
- Fever lasting more than 2 to 3 days, particularly with weakness, headache, stomach pain, or constipation or diarrhoea
- Fever after travel to, or living in, an area where typhoid is common
- Fever after eating street food or drinking untreated water, or if others around you with similar symptoms have been diagnosed with typhoid
- Fever in a young child, pregnant woman or older adult
- Persistent vomiting, dehydration, or inability to take fluids or medicines
- Fever that returns after seeming to get better
- Any warning sign in Section 6 (**go to hospital immediately**)
- Before taking antibiotics. Do not start antibiotics on your own.
- If you are a known carrier or work with food and have a history of typhoid
- To ask about typhoid vaccination before travel or for children in high-risk areas

## 13. Common Misconceptions
1. **"Typhoid and typhus are the same."** They are different diseases with different causes. Typhoid is spread through contaminated food and water; typhus is spread by lice, fleas or mites.
2. **"Typhoid spreads through the air, like a cold."** It spreads through contaminated food and water (faecal-oral route).
3. **"A positive Widal test always confirms typhoid."** The Widal test has significant limitations; doctors interpret it alongside symptoms and other tests, and blood culture is more reliable.
4. **"Typhoid always goes away on its own."** It can become serious and even fatal without proper antibiotic treatment.
5. **"I can take any antibiotic left over at home."** Wrong or incomplete antibiotics can worsen resistance and delay recovery.
6. **"I'm cured as soon as the fever goes away."** Complete the full prescribed course; relapse and the carrier state can occur.
7. **"Only dirty or poor-looking places have typhoid."** Contaminated water or food can be present anywhere, including in clean-looking settings.
8. **"Vaccinated people can't get typhoid."** Vaccines reduce risk but do not give complete protection, so safe food and water practices are still needed.
9. **"Once you've had typhoid you can never get it again."** Reinfection is possible.
10. **"Only the person with symptoms can spread it."** Carriers without symptoms can also spread the bacteria.

## 14. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is typhoid?**
Typhoid is a bacterial infection caused by *Salmonella* Typhi that spreads through contaminated food and water. It causes prolonged fever and can affect the whole body.

**Q2. How does typhoid spread?**
By swallowing food or water contaminated with the faeces or urine of an infected person or carrier. Poor sanitation and hand hygiene are major factors.

**Q3. What are the symptoms of typhoid?**
A fever that rises gradually, weakness, headache, stomach pain, loss of appetite, and constipation or diarrhoea. Some people get a faint rash on the chest or belly.

**Q4. How long after infection do symptoms appear?**
Usually about 6 to 30 days after exposure, often 1 to 3 weeks.

**Q5. What is the difference between typhoid and paratyphoid?**
Both are enteric fevers with similar symptoms. Typhoid is caused by *Salmonella* Typhi, and paratyphoid by *Salmonella* Paratyphi. Paratyphoid is often milder.

**Q6. How is typhoid diagnosed?**
Mainly by growing the bacteria from a blood sample (blood culture), and sometimes from stool, urine or bone marrow. The Widal test is limited and should be interpreted by a doctor.

**Q7. Is typhoid curable?**
Yes. Typhoid is treated with antibiotics prescribed by a doctor, and most people recover when treated promptly.

**Q8. Why is antibiotic resistance a concern in typhoid?**
Some strains no longer respond to commonly used antibiotics, including multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant strains. This is why you should take antibiotics only as prescribed, complete the course, and why testing for sensitivity matters.

**Q9. What is a typhoid carrier?**
A person who continues to carry and shed the bacteria, often without symptoms, sometimes for a long time. Carriers can spread typhoid, so doctors may advise testing, treatment, and temporary restriction from food handling.

**Q10. Can typhoid come back after treatment?**
Yes, relapse can occur in some people, usually after the fever has gone. Seek medical care if fever or symptoms return.

**Q11. How can I prevent typhoid?**
Drink safe water, eat well-cooked and safely prepared food, wash hands with soap, maintain good sanitation, and consider vaccination in high-risk areas or before travel.

**Q12. Is there a typhoid vaccine?**
Yes. WHO recommends typhoid conjugate vaccines (TCV) in high-burden countries. Other vaccines exist (Vi polysaccharide and oral Ty21a), with different age limits. Ask a doctor about which suits you or your child. The vaccine does not provide full protection, so safe food and water practices remain important.

**Q13. What should I eat and drink during typhoid?**
Take plenty of safe fluids (such as oral rehydration solution and boiled water) and easily digestible food as advised by your doctor. Food hygiene is important to protect others.

**Q14. Can I go to work or school with typhoid?**
Rest is important during illness. A doctor should advise when you can return, and food handlers and caregivers of young children should only return once cleared, with strict hand hygiene.

**Q15. Where can I get typhoid testing and treatment in India?**
At government and private health facilities, including primary health centres and hospitals. Ask at your nearest health centre or a local health worker for guidance.
